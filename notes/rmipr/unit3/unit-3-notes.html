1. Methods of Data Collection

Definition

Primary Data are gathered afresh and for the first time by the investigator, having an original character. Secondary Data are those already collected and compiled by an external agency, having passed through statistical processing.

1.1 Primary Data Collection Methods

  • Observation Method: Data gathered through direct monitoring without asking the respondent.
    • Structured vs. Unstructured: Structured defines specific observational units in advance; Unstructured monitors naturally without predefined categories.
    • Participant vs. Non-participant: In participant observation, the researcher actively joins the group being observed.
  • Interview Method: Oral verbal stimulus presented in face-to-face interaction or telephonically.
    • Structured: Uses predetermined, rigidly sequenced questions.
    • Unstructured: Flexible, conversational, exploring psychological nuances.
  • Questionnaires vs. Schedules:
    • A Questionnaire is mailed or handed to respondents to be read and answered by themselves without assistance.
    • A Schedule contains identical questions but is read aloud and filled out by a trained enumerator/investigator who visits the respondent in person.
Criterion Questionnaire Method Schedule Method
Administration Self-administered by the respondent. Filled out by trained enumerator in person.
Cost & Coverage Low cost, wide geographic reach. High cost, requires hiring and training enumerators.
Non-Response Rate High (many recipients discard forms). Very low (enumerator ensures completion).
Literacy Dependency Requires literate respondents. Applicable even for non-literate respondents.
Quick Recall

Primary data is gathered afresh (observation, interview, questionnaires, schedules). Questionnaires are self-filled by respondents; schedules are administered by trained enumerators.

2. Measurement & Scaling Techniques

Measurement is the process of mapping numbers or symbols to properties of objects according to specific rules. S.S. Stevens established four levels of measurement scales:

Scale Level Mathematical Properties Permissible Statistics Engineering Examples
1. Nominal Identity / Classification only ($A = B$ or $A \neq B$). No order or distance. Mode, Frequency, Percentage, Chi-square ($\chi^2$). Operating System type (Linux, Windows, macOS); Packet protocol (TCP, UDP, ICMP).
2. Ordinal Classification + Rank Ordering ($A > B > C$). Intervals between ranks are unequal/unknown. Median, Percentiles, Spearman's rank correlation. Software defect severity (Low, Medium, High, Critical); Likert agreement scales (1 to 5).
3. Interval Classification + Rank + Equal Intervals. Arbitrary (non-absolute) zero point. Addition & subtraction valid. Mean, Standard Deviation, Pearson's $r$, $t$-test, $F$-test. Temperature in Celsius/Fahrenheit; Calendar year (2025); Calendar time.
4. Ratio Classification + Rank + Equal Intervals + Absolute True Zero. Ratios ($A = 2B$) are physically meaningful. All statistics: Geometric mean, Harmonic mean, Coefficient of variation. Execution latency (microseconds), Memory usage (megabytes), Clock frequency (GHz), Weight.

2.1 Tests of Sound Measurement

  • Reliability: Consistency and repeatability of measurement. Assessed via Test-Retest reliability, Split-Half reliability, and Cronbach's Alpha ($\alpha \ge 0.70$).
  • Validity: Whether the instrument truly measures what it purports to measure.
    • Content Validity: Adequate coverage of all facets of the construct.
    • Criterion-Related Validity: Correlation with an external benchmark (Predictive or Concurrent).
    • Construct Validity: Alignment with underlying theoretical concepts (Convergent & Discriminant).
Quick Recall

Stevens' 4 scales: Nominal (labels) → Ordinal (ranks) → Interval (equal units, arbitrary zero) → Ratio (equal units, absolute true zero). Ratio permits multiplication and division.

3. Sampling Fundamentals & Types of Sample Design

Definition

A Sample is a finite subset of a population selected to estimate the parameters of that population. Sampling Design is the predetermined plan for obtaining a sample from a given population before any data are gathered.

3.1 Probability Sampling vs. Non-Probability Sampling

Category Technique Mechanism & Defining Feature
Probability Sampling
(Every element has a known, non-zero inclusion probability)		 Simple Random Sampling (SRS) Every item has an equal probability ($\frac{1}{N}$) of selection via lottery or random number generators.
Systematic Sampling Select every $k^{\text{th}}$ element from an ordered list after picking the first element randomly ($k = N/n$).
Stratified Random Sampling Population is partitioned into mutually exclusive, homogeneous strata (e.g. Freshmen, Sophomores, Seniors), and SRS is drawn from each stratum (Proportional or Disproportional).
Cluster Sampling Population is divided into heterogeneous naturally occurring clusters (e.g. server racks); a random sample of clusters is chosen, and all items in selected clusters are inspected.
Non-Probability Sampling
(Selection based on subjective human discretion)		 Convenience Sampling Units chosen based on immediate geographic proximity or ease of access.
Purposive / Judgment Sampling Researcher hand-picks units deemed most representative based on professional expertise.
Quota Sampling Interviewer fills specific demographic quotas (e.g. 50 female developers, 50 male developers) non-randomly.
Snowball Sampling Existing study subjects recruit future subjects from among their acquaintances (used for rare/hidden populations).
Quick Recall

Probability sampling (SRS, Systematic, Stratified, Cluster) eliminates selection bias and supports statistical inference. Non-probability (Convenience, Judgment, Quota, Snowball) relies on subjective judgment.

4. Foundations of Hypothesis Testing

Definition

A Statistical Hypothesis is an assertive, testable proposition concerning one or more unknown parameters of a population probability distribution.

4.1 Null Hypothesis ($H_0$) and Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$)

  • Null Hypothesis ($H_0$): The default hypothesis of "no difference", "no effect", or "no relationship" (e.g., $H_0: \mu = 320$, or $H_0: p = 0.20$). It is presumed innocent until proven guilty by compelling empirical evidence.
  • Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$ or $H_a$): The assertion that a real effect, difference, or relationship exists (e.g., $H_1: \mu \neq 320$, $H_1: \mu > 320$).

4.2 Decision Outcomes: Type I & Type II Errors

In evaluating a null hypothesis based on sample data, an investigator faces four possible outcomes:

True State of Nature Decision: Accept (Fail to Reject) $H_0$ Decision: Reject $H_0$ (Accept $H_1$)
$H_0$ is Actually True Correct Decision
Probability = $1 - \alpha$ (Confidence Level)		 Type I Error ($\alpha$)
"False Alarm" (Rejecting a true null hypothesis)
$H_0$ is Actually False Type II Error ($\beta$)
"Missed Detection" (Failing to reject a false null hypothesis)		 Correct Decision
Probability = $1 - \beta$ (Statistical Power of the Test)
Figure 3.1: Two-Tailed Hypothesis Testing Decision Regions ($\alpha = 0.05$)

Quick Recall

Type I Error ($\alpha$) = false alarm (rejecting true $H_0$). Type II Error ($\beta$) = missed detection (failing to reject false $H_0$). Statistical Power = $1 - \beta$.

5. The Universal 5-Step Hypothesis Testing Protocol

Every hypothesis testing exam question must strictly follow these five structured steps:

  1. Step 1: State the Hypotheses: Formulate $H_0$ (null condition of equality) and $H_1$ (alternative condition, noting whether one-tailed or two-tailed).
  2. Step 2: Specify the Significance Level ($\alpha$): Identify $\alpha$ (typically $0.05$ or $0.01$) and note the degree of confidence $1 - \alpha$.
  3. Step 3: Select and Calculate the Test Statistic: Compute $Z_{\text{cal}}$, $t_{\text{cal}}$, $\chi^2_{\text{cal}}$, or $F_{\text{cal}}$ using sample data and appropriate standard error formulas.
  4. Step 4: Determine the Critical Region (Rejection Threshold): Read critical value ($Z_{\text{crit}}$ or $t_{\text{crit}}$) from statistical tables for given $\alpha$ and degrees of freedom ($df$).
  5. Step 5: Formulate the Decision & Managerial Interpretation:
    • If the test statistic falls in the critical region ($|Z_{\text{cal}}| \ge Z_{\text{crit}}$), Reject $H_0$.
    • Otherwise, Fail to reject $H_0$ (Accept $H_0$). State the conclusion in plain language relative to the problem context.
Quick Recall

The 5 Steps: 1. State $H_0, H_1$; 2. Set $\alpha$; 3. Compute test statistic; 4. Find critical threshold; 5. Conclude (Reject or Fail to Reject $H_0$).

6. Parametric Tests ($Z$-Test & Student's $t$-Test)

6.1 $Z$-Test for Population Mean (Large Sample $n \ge 30$ or Known $\sigma$)

Formula 1: Standard $Z$-Test for Mean $$\mathbf{Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\sigma_{\bar{X}}}}$$

where $\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$ for an infinite (or very large) population.

Formula 2: Finite Population Correction Factor (FPC)

When sampling without replacement from a finite population of size $N$ such that the sampling fraction exceeds 5% ($\frac{n}{N} > 0.05$), the standard error must be multiplied by the Finite Population Correction (FPC):

$$\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right) \cdot \sqrt{\frac{N - n}{N - 1}}$$

6.2 $Z$-Test for Population Proportion

Formula 3: $Z$-Test for Single Proportion $$\mathbf{Z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{\frac{p_0 \cdot q_0}{n}}}}$$

where $p_0$ is the hypothesized proportion, $q_0 = 1 - p_0$, and $\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}$ is the sample proportion of successes.

6.3 Student's $t$-Test for Small Samples ($n < 30$, Unknown $\sigma$)

Formula 4: One-Sample Student's $t$-Test $$\mathbf{t = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}}, \quad df = n - 1}$$

where $s$ is the sample standard deviation calculated with Bessel's correction $(n - 1)$:

$$s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2}{n - 1}}$$
Quick Recall

Use $Z$-test for large samples ($n \ge 30$) or known $\sigma$. If finite population fraction $n/N > 0.05$, apply FPC $\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}}$. Use Student's $t$-test for small samples ($n < 30$) with unknown $\sigma$ ($df = n-1$).

7. Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) Test of Independence

Definition

The Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) Test of Independence is a non-parametric statistical test used to determine whether a significant association exists between two categorical variables arranged in an $r \times c$ contingency table.

Formula: Chi-Square Test Statistic $$\mathbf{\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r \sum_{j=1}^c \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}, \quad df = (r - 1)(c - 1)}$$

where $O_{ij}$ is the observed frequency in cell $(i, j)$, and $E_{ij}$ is the theoretical expected frequency under the null hypothesis of independence:

$$E_{ij} = \frac{(\text{Row } i \text{ Total}) \times (\text{Column } j \text{ Total})}{\text{Grand Total } N}$$
Validity Conditions for Chi-Square
  • Observations must be independent and collected randomly.
  • Total sample size $N$ should be reasonably large ($N \ge 50$).
  • No expected cell frequency $E_{ij}$ should be less than 5. If $E_{ij} < 5$, adjacent categories must be pooled or Yates' correction for continuity applied for $2 \times 2$ tables.
Quick Recall

$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}$ with $df = (r-1)(c-1)$. Expected frequency $E = \frac{\text{Row Total} \times \text{Col Total}}{\text{Grand Total}}$. Valid only when all $E \ge 5$.

8. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) & Covariance (ANOCOVA)

8.1 One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

While the $t$-test compares two means, ANOVA tests the equality of $k \ge 3$ population means simultaneously without inflating the Type I error rate:

$$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_k \quad \text{vs.} \quad H_1: \text{At least one mean differs}$$

ANOVA partitions total variance into two orthogonal components:

$$SS_{\text{Total}} = SS_{\text{Between}} + SS_{\text{Within}}$$ $$F = \frac{MS_{\text{Between}}}{MS_{\text{Within}}} = \frac{SS_{\text{Between}} / (k - 1)}{SS_{\text{Within}} / (N - k)}$$

8.2 Analysis of Covariance (ANOCOVA)

ANOCOVA combines ANOVA with linear regression. It removes the confounding effect of one or more uncontrolled continuous concomitant variables (covariates, $X$) that correlate with the dependent variable ($Y$), thereby reducing residual experimental error and sharpening treatment comparisons.

Quick Recall

ANOVA compares $k \ge 3$ means using the $F$-ratio ($MS_{\text{Between}} / MS_{\text{Within}}$). ANOCOVA integrates regression to statistically adjust for confounding continuous covariates.

9. Solved University Exam Numericals

Solved Exam Problem 3.1: Finite Population Mean Test (SEE Feb/Mar 2025, Q5b, 10 Marks)

Problem Statement: Suppose we are interested in a population of 20 industrial units of the same size, all of which are experiencing excessive labour turnover problems. The past records show that the mean of the distribution of annual turnover is 320 employees, with a standard deviation of 75 employees. A sample of 5 of these industrial units is taken at random which gives a mean of annual turnover as 300 employees. Is the sample mean consistent with the population mean? Test at 5% level of significance.

Step 1: Formulate Hypotheses:

  • Null Hypothesis $H_0: \mu = 320$ (Sample mean is consistent with population mean turnover).
  • Alternative Hypothesis $H_1: \mu \neq 320$ (Two-tailed test).

Step 2: Identify Parameters & Check Finite Population Condition:

  • Population size $N = 20$
  • Sample size $n = 5$
  • Hypothesized mean $\mu_0 = 320$, Population standard deviation $\sigma = 75$
  • Sample mean $\bar{X} = 300$, Significance level $\alpha = 0.05$

Check sampling fraction: $\frac{n}{N} = \frac{5}{20} = 0.25 = 25\% > 5\%$. Therefore, the Finite Population Correction (FPC) factor is strictly required!

Step 3: Calculate Standard Error with FPC:

$$\text{FPC} = \sqrt{\frac{N - n}{N - 1}} = \sqrt{\frac{20 - 5}{20 - 1}} = \sqrt{\frac{15}{19}} \approx 0.8885$$ $$\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right) \times \text{FPC} = \left(\frac{75}{\sqrt{5}}\right) \times 0.8885 = 33.541 \times 0.8885 \approx 29.8020$$

Step 4: Compute Test Statistic:

$$Z_{\text{cal}} = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\sigma_{\bar{X}}} = \frac{300 - 320}{29.8020} = \frac{-20}{29.8020} \approx \mathbf{-0.6711}$$

Step 5: Decision & Conclusion:

For a two-tailed test at $\alpha = 0.05$, the critical value is $Z_{0.025} = \pm 1.96$.

Since $|Z_{\text{cal}}| = 0.6711 < 1.96$, the test statistic falls comfortably in the non-rejection region.

$$\mathbf{|Z_{\text{cal}}| = 0.6711 < 1.96 \implies \text{Fail to Reject } H_0}$$

Conclusion: The sample mean turnover of 300 employees is statistically consistent with the population mean of 320 employees at the 5% level of significance.

Solved Exam Problem 3.2: $Z$-Test for Population Proportion (SEE Feb/Mar 2025, Q6b, 10 Marks)

Problem Statement: The null hypothesis is that 20 per cent of the passengers go in first class, but management recognizes the possibility that this percentage could be more or less. A random sample of 400 passengers includes 70 passengers holding first class tickets. Can we reject the null hypothesis at the 5% level of significance?

Step 1: Formulate Hypotheses:

  • Null Hypothesis $H_0: p = 0.20$ (20% of passengers travel in first class).
  • Alternative Hypothesis $H_1: p \neq 0.20$ (Two-tailed test).

Step 2: Sample Proportion & Standard Error:

  • Sample size $n = 400$
  • Successes $x = 70 \implies \hat{p} = \frac{70}{400} = 0.175$
  • Hypothesized proportion $p_0 = 0.20 \implies q_0 = 1 - 0.20 = 0.80$
$$\sigma_p = \sqrt{\frac{p_0 \cdot q_0}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.20 \times 0.80}{400}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.16}{400}} = \sqrt{0.0004} = 0.02$$

Step 3: Compute $Z_{\text{cal}}$:

$$Z_{\text{cal}} = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sigma_p} = \frac{0.175 - 0.20}{0.02} = \frac{-0.025}{0.02} = \mathbf{-1.25}$$

Step 4: Decision:

At $\alpha = 0.05$ (two-tailed), critical $Z = \pm 1.96$.

$$\mathbf{|Z_{\text{cal}}| = 1.25 < 1.96 \implies \text{Fail to Reject } H_0}$$

Conclusion: We cannot reject the null hypothesis. The observed difference ($17.5\%$ vs $20\%$) is attributable to chance sampling variation at the 5% level.

Solved Exam Problem 3.3: Small Sample Student's $t$-Test (Makeup Apr 2025, Q6b, 10 Marks)

Problem Statement: The average adult male height in a certain country is 170 cm. We suspect that the men in a certain city in that country might have a different average height due to environmental factors. We pick a random sample of size 9 from the adult males in the city and obtain the following values for their heights (in cm):
176.2, 157.9, 160.1, 180.9, 165.1, 167.2, 162.9, 155.7, 166.2.
Test whether average male height can be 170 cm. Consider significance level to be 5%.

Step 1: State Hypotheses:

  • $H_0: \mu = 170$ cm (Average male height in the city is 170 cm).
  • $H_1: \mu \neq 170$ cm (Two-tailed test).

Step 2: Compute Sample Mean ($\bar{X}$) and Sample Standard Deviation ($s$):

$$\sum X = 176.2 + 157.9 + 160.1 + 180.9 + 165.1 + 167.2 + 162.9 + 155.7 + 166.2 = 1492.2$$ $$\bar{X} = \frac{1492.2}{9} = \mathbf{165.80 \text{ cm}}$$

Deviations $(X_i - \bar{X})$ and squared deviations $(X_i - \bar{X})^2$:

  • $176.2 - 165.8 = +10.4 \implies (10.4)^2 = 108.16$
  • $157.9 - 165.8 = -7.9 \implies (-7.9)^2 = 62.41$
  • $160.1 - 165.8 = -5.7 \implies (-5.7)^2 = 32.49$
  • $180.9 - 165.8 = +15.1 \implies (15.1)^2 = 228.01$
  • $165.1 - 165.8 = -0.7 \implies (-0.7)^2 = 0.49$
  • $167.2 - 165.8 = +1.4 \implies (1.4)^2 = 1.96$
  • $162.9 - 165.8 = -2.9 \implies (-2.9)^2 = 8.41$
  • $155.7 - 165.8 = -10.1 \implies (-10.1)^2 = 102.01$
  • $166.2 - 165.8 = +0.4 \implies (0.4)^2 = 0.16$
$$\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2 = 108.16 + 62.41 + 32.49 + 228.01 + 0.49 + 1.96 + 8.41 + 102.01 + 0.16 = 544.10$$ $$s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2}{n - 1}} = \sqrt{\frac{544.10}{8}} = \sqrt{68.0125} \approx \mathbf{8.2470 \text{ cm}}$$

Step 3: Calculate Student's $t$ Statistic:

$$\text{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{8.2470}{\sqrt{9}} = \frac{8.2470}{3} \approx 2.7490$$ $$t_{\text{cal}} = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\text{SE}} = \frac{165.80 - 170.00}{2.7490} = \frac{-4.20}{2.7490} \approx \mathbf{-1.5278}$$

Step 4: Critical Value and Decision:

Degrees of freedom $df = n - 1 = 9 - 1 = 8$.

From the Student's $t$-table, two-tailed critical $t$ for $df = 8$ at $\alpha = 0.05$ is $t_{0.025, 8} = \mathbf{\pm 2.306}$.

$$\mathbf{|t_{\text{cal}}| = 1.5278 < 2.306 \implies \text{Fail to Reject } H_0}$$

Conclusion: We accept $H_0$. There is insufficient statistical evidence at the 5% level to reject the claim that average adult male height in the city is 170 cm.

Solved Problem 3.4: Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) Test of Independence on $2 \times 2$ Table

Problem Statement: A survey evaluated 100 software repositories to test whether defect rates depend on development methodology (Agile vs. Waterfall). The observed counts are:

Methodology High Defect (> 10) Low Defect (<= 10) Total
Agile153550
Waterfall302050
Total4555100

Test at the 5% level ($\alpha = 0.05$) whether defect rate is independent of development methodology.

Step 1: Compute Expected Frequencies ($E = \frac{\text{Row Total} \times \text{Col Total}}{\text{Grand Total}}$):

  • $E_{11} = \frac{50 \times 45}{100} = 22.5$
  • $E_{12} = \frac{50 \times 55}{100} = 27.5$
  • $E_{21} = \frac{50 \times 45}{100} = 22.5$
  • $E_{22} = \frac{50 \times 55}{100} = 27.5$

Step 2: Calculate $\chi^2_{\text{cal}}$:

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(15 - 22.5)^2}{22.5} + \frac{(35 - 27.5)^2}{27.5} + \frac{(30 - 22.5)^2}{22.5} + \frac{(20 - 27.5)^2}{27.5}$$ $$\chi^2 = \frac{(-7.5)^2}{22.5} + \frac{(7.5)^2}{27.5} + \frac{(7.5)^2}{22.5} + \frac{(-7.5)^2}{27.5} = \frac{56.25}{22.5} + \frac{56.25}{27.5} + \frac{56.25}{22.5} + \frac{56.25}{27.5}$$ $$\chi^2 = 2.5000 + 2.0455 + 2.5000 + 2.0455 = \mathbf{9.0910}$$

Step 3: Degrees of Freedom & Critical Value:

$$df = (r - 1)(c - 1) = (2 - 1)(2 - 1) = 1$$

Critical $\chi^2_{0.05, 1} = \mathbf{3.841}$.

$$\mathbf{\chi^2_{\text{cal}} = 9.0910 > 3.841 \implies \text{Reject } H_0}$$

Conclusion: Reject $H_0$. There is a statistically significant association between development methodology and defect rates ($p < 0.01$).

Ramaiah Autonomous Examination • CIE-2 (Dec 22, 2025) Q1.a [8 Marks]

"Compare and contrast the observation method and interview method of primary data collection."

Official Model Solution:

Comparison Dimension Observation Method Interview Method
Mode of Data Capture Direct monitoring and recording of behavior without asking respondents. Oral verbal interaction (face-to-face or telephone) with direct questioning.
Subjectivity & Bias Eliminates respondent self-report bias; observer bias is controlled via structured observation protocols. Susceptible to social desirability bias from respondents and interviewer tone/probing bias.
Depth of Internal Insight Limited strictly to observable external actions; cannot probe underlying thoughts, past history, or private motives. Deep psychological probing into past experiences, motivations, emotional nuances, and attitudes.
Respondent Cooperation Does not require active respondent willingness; ideal for automated sensors, animals, or unconscious patients. Requires full active cooperation, availability, and articulacy of the interviewee.
Cost & Time Expensive per unit of data captured due to prolonged idle waiting for spontaneous events. Higher throughput per appointment; scheduling overhead and geographic travel costs can be substantial.
Ramaiah Autonomous Examination • CIE-2 (Dec 22, 2025) Q3.b [7 Marks]

"Explain the steps involved in sample design."

Official Model Solution: Developing an authentic sample design follows six structured steps (C.R. Kothari Ch. 4):

  1. Type of Universe (Target Population): Define whether the universe of study is finite (e.g. 5,000 enrolled students) or infinite (e.g. continuous network packet streams).
  2. Sampling Unit: Decide the basic operational unit to be sampled before selecting items (geographical units like states/districts, institutional units like schools, or individual persons).
  3. Source List (Sampling Frame): Secure the authoritative register, roster, or directory containing every sampling unit from which the sample is drawn (e.g. university registrar enrollment database).
  4. Sample Size ($n$): Calculate the mathematically optimal number of items ensuring adequate precision, confidence level, and acceptable margin of error within budget constraints.
  5. Budgetary Constraints: Balance statistical variance reduction against real-world limitations of cost, compute time, and field investigator manpower.
  6. Sampling Procedure: Select the exact probability sampling method (Simple Random, Stratified, Systematic, Cluster, or Multi-stage) that minimizes sampling error.
Ramaiah Autonomous Examination • SEE (Feb/Mar 2025) Q5.b [10 Marks]

"Suppose we are interested in a population of 20 industrial units of the same size, all of which are experiencing excessive labour turnover problems. The past records show that the mean annual turnover is 320 employees, with a standard deviation of 75 employees. A sample of 5 of these units is taken at random which gives a mean turnover of 300 employees. Is the sample mean consistent with the population mean? Test at 5% level."

Official Model Solution:

See Solved Exam Problem 3.1 above for the full step-by-step derivation:

  • $N = 20, n = 5 \implies \frac{n}{N} = 0.25 > 0.05 \implies$ FPC factor required!
  • $\text{FPC} = \sqrt{\frac{20-5}{20-1}} = \sqrt{\frac{15}{19}} \approx 0.8885$
  • $\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \left(\frac{75}{\sqrt{5}}\right) \times 0.8885 = 29.8020$
  • $Z_{\text{cal}} = \frac{300 - 320}{29.8020} = \mathbf{-0.6711}$
  • Critical value for two-tailed test at $\alpha = 0.05$ is $Z_{\text{crit}} = \pm 1.96$.
  • Since $|-0.6711| < 1.96$, Fail to reject $H_0$. The sample turnover of 300 is consistent with the population mean of 320.
Ramaiah Autonomous Examination • SEE (Feb/Mar 2025) Q6.b [10 Marks]

"The null hypothesis is that 20 per cent of passengers go in first class, but management recognizes the possibility that this percentage could be more or less. A random sample of 400 passengers includes 70 holding first class tickets. Can the null hypothesis be rejected at the 5% level of significance?"

Official Model Solution:

  • $H_0: p = 0.20, \quad H_1: p \ne 0.20$
  • $n = 400, x = 70 \implies \hat{p} = \frac{70}{400} = 0.175$
  • $\sigma_p = \sqrt{\frac{0.20 \times 0.80}{400}} = \sqrt{0.0004} = 0.02$
  • $Z_{\text{cal}} = \frac{0.175 - 0.20}{0.02} = \mathbf{-1.25}$
  • $|Z_{\text{cal}}| = 1.25 < 1.96 \implies$ Fail to reject $H_0$.

A teacher believes 85% of students want a zoo trip ($p_0 = 0.85$). A sample of 50 students yields 39 wanting to go. Test at $\alpha = 0.05$ whether the proportion differs from 85%.

PRACTICE 2 • STUDENT'S t-TEST ON HEIGHTS

A sample of 9 adult males yields heights: 176.2, 157.9, 160.1, 180.9, 165.1, 167.2, 162.9, 155.7, 166.2. Test at $\alpha = 0.05$ whether the population mean is 170 cm.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Sample mean $\bar{X} = 165.80\text{ cm}$, sample standard deviation $s = 8.2470\text{ cm}$.

$$\text{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{8.2470}{3} = 2.7490$$ $$t_{\text{cal}} = \frac{165.80 - 170.0}{2.7490} = \mathbf{-1.5278}$$

At $df = 8$ and $\alpha = 0.05$, critical $t_{0.025, 8} = 2.306$. Since $|-1.5278| < 2.306$, Fail to reject $H_0$. Average male height can be 170 cm. (Verified in audit/verify/rmipr/verify_rmipr_u3.py).

PRACTICE 3 • CHI-SQUARE TEST OF INDEPENDENCE

In a $2 \times 2$ contingency table with observed counts $O = [15, 35, 30, 20]$ and equal marginal totals ($50, 50, 45, 55$), compute the calculated $\chi^2$ value and evaluate at $\alpha = 0.05$.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Expected frequencies: $E = [22.5, 27.5, 22.5, 27.5]$.

$$\chi^2 = \frac{(15-22.5)^2}{22.5} + \frac{(35-27.5)^2}{27.5} + \frac{(30-22.5)^2}{22.5} + \frac{(20-27.5)^2}{27.5} = 2.5 + 2.0455 + 2.5 + 2.0455 = \mathbf{9.091}$$

At $df = 1$, critical $\chi^2_{0.05, 1} = 3.841$. Since $9.091 > 3.841$, Reject $H_0$. (Verified in audit/verify/rmipr/verify_rmipr_u3.py).

PRACTICE 5 • TYPE I VS TYPE II ERROR TRADEOFF

Explain what happens to the probability of committing a Type II Error ($\beta$) when an investigator reduces the significance level ($\alpha$) from 0.05 to 0.01.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

For a fixed sample size $n$, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are inversely related. Reducing $\alpha$ from 0.05 to 0.01 makes the critical boundary more stringent, decreasing the probability of a false alarm (Type I error). However, this expands the non-rejection region, increasing the probability of committing a Type II Error ($\beta$) and lowering statistical test power ($1 - \beta$).

PRACTICE 6 • ANOVA F-RATIO INTERPRETATION

In an ANOVA test with $k = 4$ groups and total $N = 40$ units, what are the numerator and denominator degrees of freedom for the $F$-statistic?

Show Step-by-Step Solution
  • Numerator $df_{\text{Between}} = k - 1 = 4 - 1 = \mathbf{3}$
  • Denominator $df_{\text{Within}} = N - k = 40 - 4 = \mathbf{36}$
  • Total $df = N - 1 = 39$. Sum check: $3 + 36 = 39$.
