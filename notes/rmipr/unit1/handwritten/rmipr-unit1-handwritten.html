AL58 · Unit 1 2. Objectives & Motivations in Research

1. Meaning & Nature of Research

Definition:

Research is a systematic, organized, and objective investigation undertaken to establish facts, solve problems, test hypotheses, or discover new knowledge through the rigorous application of the scientific method.

Etymologically, research comprises two Latin-derived parts: "Re" (meaning again, anew, or afresh) and "Search" (meaning to examine closely and carefully, or to test and try). It is fundamentally a movement from the known to the unknown—a voyage of discovery.

1.1 Research vs. Random Exploration

Unsystematic trial-and-error differs drastically from scientific research. Research demands:

  • Systematization: Planned, structured execution following established methodological protocols.
  • Empirical Evidence: Reliance on observable, measurable phenomena rather than superstition, tradition, or unverified claims.
  • Reproducibility (Replicability): Independent researchers following identical procedures under equivalent constraints must obtain commensurate findings.
  • Controlled Conditions: Isolating causal relationships by moderating extraneous variables.

2. Objectives & Motivations in Research

The principal objective of research is to unearth answers to questions through scientific procedures that prevent subjective human bias from corrupting the truth.

2.1 Four Broad Classes of Research Objectives

  1. Exploratory / Formulative: To achieve familiarity with an unfamiliar phenomenon or achieve new insights (e.g., preliminary pilot study into an emerging zero-day cybersecurity vulnerability).
  2. Descriptive: To accurately portray the characteristics of a particular individual, situation, community, or software architecture (e.g., census surveys, measuring average developer turnover rate in Bangalore tech firms).
  3. Diagnostic: To establish the frequency with which an event occurs or with which it associates with another condition (e.g., investigating why database latency spikes sharply during flash sales).
  4. Hypothesis-Testing (Causal): To test a causal hypothesis asserting a definitive relationship between dependent and independent variables (e.g., testing whether increasing CPU cache from 16MB to 32MB significantly reduces inference latency of a transformer model).

2.2 What Motivates People to Undertake Research?

  • Intellectual Curiosity: The desire to understand the unexplained mechanics of natural or computational phenomena.
  • Solving Practical Challenges: The urge to resolve societal, environmental, or engineering bottlenecks (e.g., developing low-power IoT MAC protocols).
  • Academic & Professional Growth: Earning an advanced research degree (M.S., Ph.D.) or gaining intellectual recognition among peers.
  • Social Responsibility & Utility: Directing technical innovation to elevate human well-being and public safety.
Quick Recall:

Four objective pillars: Exploratory (new insights), Descriptive (profiling traits), Diagnostic (frequency & association), and Hypothesis-testing (causal verification).

4. Ethics in Research

Research ethics provides the moral compass governing all scholarly inquiry, data curation, experimental treatment, and publication dissemination.

4.1 Foundational Ethical Principles

  • Honesty: Honestly report data, results, methods, and publication status. Never deceive colleagues, grant sponsors, or the public.
  • Objectivity: Eliminate bias in experimental design, data interpretation, peer review, personnel decisions, and expert testimony.
  • Integrity: Keep agreements and promises; act with sincerity; strive for consistency of thought and action.
  • Carefulness: Avoid careless errors and negligence; examine your work and colleagues' work with rigorous scrutiny; maintain spotless logs.
  • Openness: Share data, results, tools, resources, and ideas openly. Remain receptive to novel ideas and healthy criticism.
  • Respect for Intellectual Property: Honor patents, copyrights, trademarks, trade secrets, and licenses. Never use unpublished data without prior authorization. Always give credit where credit is due.
  • Confidentiality: Safeguard confidential communications (e.g., papers submitted for blind peer review, grant proposals, patient health records, industrial trade secrets).
  • Social Responsibility: Strive to promote social good and prevent harm through research, public education, and advocacy.
Quick Recall:

Ethical research rests on the bedrock of Honesty, Objectivity, Carefulness, Openness, and unconditional respect for Intellectual Property.

5. Types of Research Misconduct (The FFP Triad & Beyond)

Core Triad:

In international scientific consensus (including US Office of Research Integrity and Indian UGC/AICTE regulations), research misconduct is explicitly defined as Fabrication, Falsification, or Plagiarism (FFP) in proposing, performing, or reviewing research, or in reporting research results.

Misconduct Type Formal Definition Concrete Engineering Example
Fabrication Inventing data, synthetic observations, or fictitious experiments and recording/reporting them as genuine real-world trials. Simulating 1,000 sensor battery runs in an Excel sheet by typing random discharge numbers instead of running physical field tests.
Falsification Manipulating research materials, equipment, or processes, or changing/omitting data points such that research is not accurately represented in record. Discarding 14 latency outliers because they violate an algorithm's claimed sub-millisecond execution bound.
Plagiarism Appropriation of another person's ideas, processes, results, code, or text without giving appropriate attribution or credit. Pasting a competitor's neural network architecture and mathematical formulation into your paper without citation.

5.2 Secondary Forms of Ethical Malpractice

  • Self-Plagiarism (Duplicate Publication): Re-publishing one's own previously copyrighted text, figures, or findings in a new venue without clear disclosure or citation.
  • Salami Slicing (Data Fragmentation): Partitioning a coherent, unified large study into thin, minimally publishable units solely to artificially inflate publication counts.
  • Gift / Honorary Authorship: Adding the name of a senior professor, department chair, or influential acquaintance to an author list when they contributed no intellectual work.
  • Ghost Authorship: Concealing the true author (e.g., an industrial ghostwriter hired by a pharmaceutical firm) to mask bias.
  • Predatory Publishing: Submitting manuscripts to commercial pay-to-publish scam journals that bypass authentic peer review for financial gain.

6. Literature Review & Analysis of Prior Art

A literature review is not a superficial bibliography; it is an analytical synthesis of published scholarship establishing the context and justification for your investigation.

6.1 Interplay of New and Existing Knowledge

Scientific advancement is cumulative. Sir Isaac Newton famously observed: "If I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of Giants."

  • Grounding: Existing knowledge provides validated methodologies, established theoretical models, and standard benchmarks.
  • De-duplication: Prevents "reinventing the wheel" by ensuring researchers do not replicate solved problems.
  • Gap Identification: Highlighting limitations, contradictory findings, unexamined boundary conditions, or scalability bottlenecks in the existing literature.
  • Synthesizing: Connecting disjoint findings from distinct disciplines to construct a novel overarching framework.

7. Critical and Creative Reading in Research

Technical reading demands a higher cognitive engagement than casual reading. Researchers must read on two intertwined planes: critically and creatively.

Attribute Critical Reading (Analytical Lens) Creative Reading (Generative Lens)
Primary Mindset Skeptical, evaluating, dissecting validity and soundness. Visionary, connecting dots, exploring possibilities.
Guiding Questions "Are the assumptions justified? Are the statistics rigorous? Does the conclusion truly follow from the evidence?" "What if we relaxed assumption X? Can this technique be applied to an entirely different engineering domain?"
Focus Areas Threats to validity, unstated biases, confounding variables. Untapped cross-pollinations, novel architectural hybridizations.
Outcome Accurate judgment of the paper's actual scientific contribution. Conceptualizing your own innovative research hypothesis.
Quick Recall:

Critical reading validates the soundness and rigor of an author's claims; creative reading envisions novel extensions, adaptations, and future directions.

8. Bibliographic Databases & Metrics (hhh-index, JIF)

Reliable scholarly literature must be discovered through curated, indexed bibliographic repositories rather than generic commercial search engines.

8.1 Leading Bibliographic Repositories

  • Web of Science (WoS): Maintained by Clarivate Analytics; the oldest citation indexing service (originating from Eugene Garfield's Science Citation Index). Highly curated, covers high-impact journals, calculates the official Journal Citation Reports (JCR) Impact Factor.
  • Scopus: Maintained by Elsevier; comprehensive multidisciplinary abstract and citation database indexing over 25,000 peer-reviewed journals, book series, and conference proceedings. Generates CiteScore and SJR metrics.
  • IEEE Xplore: Digital library dedicated to electrical engineering, electronics, computer science, and telecommunications. High relevance for engineering students.

9. Citations, Knowledge Flow & Attributions

A citation is a formal reference linking an author's claim to an earlier published work, forming the connective tissue of human intellectual heritage.

9.1 Functions and Attributes of Citations

  • Verification & Evidence: Allows critical peers to audit the empirical validity and context of claims.
  • Intellectual Property & Priority: Acknowledges who first conceived an idea, established a theorem, or discovered a compound.
  • Navigational Roadmap: Directs readers toward deeper technical treatments and foundational proofs.
  • Defensive Intellectual Property: Demonstrates prior art when defending against invalid patent filings.

9.2 Impact of Title and Keywords on Citation Velocity

In modern automated indexing systems, the title and author keywords dictate discovery:

  • Title Specificity: Concise, declarative, and informative titles attract significantly higher click-through and download rates than vague or poetic phrasing.

  • Keywords Optimization: Choosing standard MeSH / IEEE taxonomy keywords ensures that search algorithms surface the manuscript in automated queries.
  • Accessibility Factor: If indexing spiders fail to match metadata to active researcher searches, even groundbreaking theorems remain uncited.

Knowledge diffuses across geography and academic disciplines via directed citation graphs. When Paper CCC cites Paper BBB, which cited foundational Paper AAA, intellectual concepts propagate outward, spawning commercial patents, technical standards, and follow-on breakthroughs.

Ramaiah Autonomous Examination • CIE-1 (Oct 29, 2025) Q3.a [8 Marks]

"What is a plagiarism? Illustrate different types of plagiarisms."

Official Model Solution:

Plagiarism is the uncredited appropriation, theft, or reproduction of another person's ideas, processes, results, words, or artistic expressions without proper formal citation and attribution, representing them as one's own original contribution.

  1. Direct / Verbatim Plagiarism: Word-for-word transcription of a section of someone else's text without quotation marks and without attribution. This is the most severe ethical breach.
  2. Mosaic / Patchwriting Plagiarism: Borrowing phrases or sentences from multiple sources and weaving them together with minor synonym replacements, retaining original sentence structures without quotation marks.
  3. Self-Plagiarism (Text Recycling): Reusing significant portions of one's own previously published copyright-protected papers in a new manuscript without disclosure or citation.
  4. Accidental / Inadvertent Plagiarism: Failing to cite sources properly due to careless note-taking, misattributing citations, or paraphrasing too closely without malicious intent (still penalized as misconduct).

(a) Research Process: 8-step iterative cycle: (1) Problem Formulation → (2) Literature Survey → (3) Hypothesis Formulation (H0,H1H_0, H_1H0​,H1​) → (4) Research Design → (5) Data Collection → (6) Data Analysis → (7) Hypothesis Testing → (8) Generalization & Report. (Refer to Figure 1.1).

(b) Descriptive vs Analytical: Descriptive research surveys current state without manipulation (e.g. measuring student survey responses on AI tool adoption). Analytical research manipulates variables to establish causal relationships (e.g. controlled A/B testing of compiler optimization flags on binary size).

PRACTICE 5 • RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS CRITERIA

State the four essential criteria of a valid scientific research hypothesis according to C.R. Kothari.

📝 Practice Problem:
Show Step-by-Step Solution
Solution:
  1. Clear and Precise: Concepts must be unambiguously defined.
  2. Capable of Verification (Falsifiability): Must be testable against empirical evidence.
  3. States Relationship Between Variables: Expresses tentative causal connection between independent and dependent variables.
  4. Consistent with Known Body of Facts: Aligns with established theoretical knowledge unless explicitly challenging it.
