1. Need & Features of Research Design

Definition:

A Research Design is the conceptual blueprint, master plan, or architectural framework within which research is conducted. It outlines the strategy for data collection, measurement, and statistical analysis to answer the research questions while maximizing empirical control.

1.1 Why is Research Design Necessary?

  • Resource Optimization: Prevents wasteful expenditures of time, funding, compute cycles, and human labour by defining precise data boundaries.
  • Reliable Causal Inference: Ensures that observed effects in the dependent variable can be unequivocally attributed to the independent variable rather than noise.
  • Operational Guidance: Provides a standardized, reproducible roadmap for investigators and laboratory assistants.
  • Flaw Detection: Enables peer reviewers and committee members to identify conceptual shortcomings before expensive experimental runs begin.

1.2 Features of a Good Research Design

According to C.R. Kothari, a robust research design demonstrates four hallmark features:

  1. Objectivity: Observational procedures and measurement scales yield identical data irrespective of the observer's subjective biases.
  2. Reliability: Consistency of measurement tools and stability of observations over repeated trials.
  3. Internal and External Validity:
    • Internal Validity: The extent to which extraneous variables are controlled so that the observed difference is strictly caused by the treatment.
    • External Validity: The generalizability of the findings across wider populations, operating environments, or hardware architectures.
  4. Economy & Flexibility: Maximizes statistical information gained per unit of cost while retaining adaptability to unexpected technical constraints.
Quick Recall:

Research design is the architectural blueprint of an investigation. Good design balances Objectivity, Reliability, Validity (Internal & External), and Economy.

2. Variables, Controls & Confounded Relationships

In quantitative and experimental research, phenomena are operationalized as variables possessing measurable numerical values.

Concept Formal Definition Engineering Benchmark Example
Independent Variable (IV) The antecedent factor manipulated or categorized by the researcher to determine its causal effect. Cache allocation size (8MB, 16MB, 32MB) in an embedded Linux system.
Dependent Variable (DV) The consequent factor measured by the researcher to evaluate the influence of the IV. Memory read/write access latency (nanoseconds).
Extraneous Variable Unwanted background factors not of direct interest that could influence the DV if left unchecked. Ambient operating temperature of the CPU or background OS scheduling daemon load.
Confounded Relationship A condition where an extraneous variable varies systematically alongside the IV, rendering it impossible to isolate which factor caused the change in the DV. Testing 8MB cache on an Intel Core i5 and 32MB cache on an AMD Ryzen 9—cache size is hopelessly confounded with processor microarchitecture!
Experimental & Control Groups Experimental: Receives the novel treatment (e.g. new scheduling algorithm).
Control: Receives the baseline or standard condition (e.g. standard Linux CFS scheduler).		 Benchmarking custom TCP BBRv3 congestion control (Experimental) vs. traditional TCP Cubic (Control) under 100ms delay.
Treatments The specific conditions or variable levels applied to experimental units. Four distinct database indexing algorithms: AAA (B-Tree), BBB (LSM-Tree), CCC (Hash Index), DDD (Bitmap).

Common Mistake:

Assuming that extraneous variables can simply be ignored if the sample size is large. Randomization disperses unknown nuisance factors, but known systematic extraneous factors must be explicitly controlled through blocking or laboratory isolation!

Quick Recall:

IV = manipulated cause; DV = measured effect; Extraneous = uncontrolled nuisance. When an extraneous variable correlates with the IV, confounding destroys internal validity.

3. Principles of Experimental Design

Formulated by Sir Ronald A. Fisher, the three cardinal principles of modern experimental design form the theoretical foundation for all controlled testing:

Figure 2.1: The Three Cardinal Principles of Experimental Design (R.A. Fisher)
Replication yields an estimate of error; Randomization ensures statistical validity; Local Control isolates nuisance variation to heighten test sensitivity.
  1. Principle of Replication: Applying each treatment to multiple independent experimental units. Replication provides an empirical estimate of random experimental error variance (σ2\sigma^2σ2) and increases the precision (power) of sample mean estimates by reducing the standard error (σ/n\sigma / \sqrt{n}σ/n​).
  2. Principle of Randomization: Assigning treatments to experimental units strictly by a random mechanism. This provides insurance against systematic investigator bias and ensures the validity of statistical significance tests (FFF-test, ttt-test).
  3. Principle of Local Control (Blocking): Grouping homogeneous experimental units into "blocks" to partition out known extraneous variation. By isolating block-to-block differences into a separate sum of squares (SSBlocksSS_{\text{Blocks}}SSBlocks​), the residual error sum of squares (SSErrorSS_{\text{Error}}SSError​) is dramatically minimized, boosting statistical power.

4. Completely Randomized Design (CRD)

Definition:

A Completely Randomized Design (CRD) is the simplest formal experimental design in which treatments are assigned to all experimental units completely at random, without grouping or blocking.

CRD applies the principles of Replication and Randomization, but completely ignores Local Control. It is appropriate exclusively when experimental units are strictly homogeneous (e.g. identical factory-calibrated microcontrollers operating under constant laboratory temperature).

4.2 Advantages and Disadvantages of CRD

  • Advantages: Maximum degrees of freedom available for experimental error; complete layout flexibility (treatments can have unequal replications); extremely simple computational analysis.
  • Disadvantages: Low precision if experimental units are heterogeneous, as all environmental variability gets dumped into the error term (SSESS_{\text{E}}SSE​), inflating the denominator and masking real treatment differences.

5. Randomized Block Design (RBD)

Definition:

A Randomized Block Design (RBD) is an experimental design where experimental units are grouped into homogeneous clusters called blocks based on an extraneous nuisance factor. Every treatment is assigned randomly within each block exactly once.

RBD incorporates all three principles: Replication (across blocks), Randomization (within blocks), and Local Control (blocking the single nuisance factor).

5.2 Advantages and Disadvantages of RBD

  • Advantages: Dramatically more precise than CRD because block-to-block variability is extracted from the error term; missing data can be estimated using Yates' formula; intuitive to design.
  • Disadvantages: If blocks are not homogeneous within themselves, error variance increases; cannot control more than one extraneous variable simultaneously.
Quick Recall:

RBD blocks one nuisance factor. dfdfdf: Blocks = b−1b-1b−1, Treatments = t−1t-1t−1, Error = (b−1)(t−1)(b-1)(t-1)(b−1)(t−1), Total = bt−1bt-1bt−1. Substantially sharper FFF-test than CRD.

6. Latin Square Design (LSD)

Definition:

A Latin Square Design (LSD) is a two-factor blocking design that simultaneously eliminates two independent sources of extraneous variation by arranging mmm treatments in an m×mm \times mm×m square matrix such that every treatment appears exactly once in each row and exactly once in each column.

In computer systems engineering, Latin Squares are deployed when experiments face two confounding nuisance factors—such as varying CPU architectures (Rows) and varying Network Congestion levels (Columns) across mmm routing protocols (Treatments).

6.2 Trade-Offs of Latin Square Design

  • Advantages: Controls two sources of extraneous variation simultaneously without requiring full factorial combinations; achieves high precision with relatively few experimental runs (m2m^2m2 instead of m3m^3m3).
  • Disadvantages: Rigid constraint that number of rows, number of columns, and number of treatments must all equal mmm; assumes zero interaction between rows, columns, and treatments; for m<4m < 4m<4, error degrees of freedom become dangerously small (m=3  ⟹  dfE=2m=3 \implies df_E = 2m=3⟹dfE​=2).

7. Factorial Designs (2k2^k2k)

Definition:

A Factorial Design is an experimental strategy in which two or more factors are investigated simultaneously across all possible combinations of their levels. A design with kkk factors each at 2 levels (low and high) is denoted as a 2k2^k2k factorial design.

7.1 Main Effects vs. Interaction Effects

Traditional "One-Factor-at-a-Time" (OFAT) testing fails to detect interactions—situations where the effect of one factor depends on the setting of another factor.

  • Main Effect of Factor AAA: The average change in response produced by moving Factor AAA from its low level (−-−) to high level (+++).
  • Interaction Effect (A×BA \times BA×B): When the magnitude or direction of AAA's effect differs drastically depending on whether BBB is low or high (e.g. increasing thread count speeds up processing only when database cache is high, but causes cache-thrashing slowdowns when cache is low!).
Quick Recall:

Factorial designs test all combination pairs, uncovering interaction effects (A×BA \times BA×B) that standard One-Factor-at-a-Time (OFAT) experiments miss.

8. Sample Size Determination

A pivotal decision in sampling and experimental design is calculating the minimum sample size (nnn) required to guarantee that sample estimates stay within an allowable margin of error (EEE) at a specified confidence level (1−α1 - \alpha1−α).

Formula 1: Sample Size for Estimating a Proportion:
n=Z2⋅p⋅qE2\mathbf{n = \frac{Z^2 \cdot p \cdot q}{E^2}}n=E2Z2⋅p⋅q​

where:

  • ZZZ: Critical value from standard normal distribution corresponding to confidence level (Z=1.96Z=1.96Z=1.96 for 95%, Z=2.576Z=2.576Z=2.576 for 99%).
  • ppp: Estimated proportion of the population possessing the characteristic. When unknown, use p=0.5p=0.5p=0.5 (which maximizes p⋅q=0.25p \cdot q = 0.25p⋅q=0.25, giving the most conservative, safe sample size).
  • q=1−pq = 1 - pq=1−p: Complementary proportion.

n=Z2⋅σ2E2\mathbf{n = \frac{Z^2 \cdot \sigma^2}{E^2}}n=E2Z2⋅σ2​

"What is research hypothesis? Explain the types of hypotheses."

Official Model Solution:

  1. Definition: A research hypothesis is a tentative, testable propositional statement postulating an expected causal relationship between two or more variables. It provides directional focus to empirical investigations.
  2. Types of Hypotheses:
    • Null Hypothesis (H0H_0H0​): Posits that there is no true difference or no significant effect between treatments; any observed variance is purely attributable to random sampling error (e.g. H0:μ1=μ2H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2H0​:μ1​=μ2​).
    • Alternative Hypothesis (H1H_1H1​ or HaH_aHa​): Represents the operational research claim that a genuine difference or treatment effect exists (H1:μ1≠μ2H_1: \mu_1 \ne \mu_2H1​:μ1​=μ2​).
    • Directional (One-tailed) Hypothesis: Specifies the exact direction of the expected difference (e.g. H1:μnew>μoldH_1: \mu_{\text{new}} > \mu_{\text{old}}H1​:μnew​>μold​).
    • Non-Directional (Two-tailed) Hypothesis: Posits that a difference exists without specifying which group will be higher or lower (H1:μ1≠μ2H_1: \mu_1 \ne \mu_2H1​:μ1​=μ2​).

"What is the role of an experimental design? Explain the basic principles of research design."

Official Model Solution:

Role of Experimental Design: Provides a systematic framework to manipulate independent variables while controlling extraneous factors, thereby minimizing experimental error and enabling valid statistical testing (FFF-test in ANOVA).

Three Basic Principles of Experimentation (R.A. Fisher):

  1. Principle of Replication: The repetition of the basic treatments under study across multiple experimental units. It provides an estimate of experimental error variance and increases the precision of treatment effect estimates.
  2. Principle of Randomization: The allocation of treatments to experimental units strictly by a random mechanism (e.g. lottery or random number table). It guarantees that observations are statistically independent and protects against experimenter selection bias.
  3. Principle of Local Control: Stratifying heterogeneous experimental units into homogeneous groups or blocks. By separating block-to-block variability from the experimental error, local control drastically reduces residual error and enhances the sensitivity of the FFF-test.

PRACTICE 4 • EXTRANEOUS VS CONFOUNDING VARIABLES

Distinguish between an extraneous variable and a confounding variable with an example from cloud benchmarking.

📝 Practice Problem:
Show Step-by-Step Solution
Solution:

Extraneous Variable: An external variable that is not the primary focus of the experiment but can introduce noise into the dependent variable (e.g. ambient network latency during API benchmarking).

Confounding Variable: An extraneous variable that correlates with both the independent variable and the dependent variable, making it impossible to disentangle their separate causal contributions (e.g. benchmark run A was tested on newer CPU hardware while benchmark run B was tested on older hardware; hardware was confounded with algorithm performance).

PRACTICE 6 • EXPERIMENTAL VS CONTROL GROUPS

Why must the experimental group and control group be treated identically in every respect except for the experimental variable?

📝 Practice Problem:
Show Step-by-Step Solution
Solution:

To preserve internal validity. If the two groups differ in any baseline condition (e.g. participant experience, hardware capability, background noise), any observed outcome difference cannot be causally attributed to the treatment, destroying the validity of the experiment.
