Define Data Communication and discuss its fundamental characteristics. With a neat diagram illustrate the five components of data communication.

Model Exam Answer

1. Definition of Data Communication

Data communication is the exchange of data between two devices via some form of transmission medium (such as a wire cable or wireless link). For data communication to occur, communicating devices must be part of a communication system made up of a combination of hardware and software.

2. Four Fundamental Characteristics

  • Delivery: The system must deliver data to the correct destination. Data must be received only by the intended device or user.
  • Accuracy: The system must deliver the data accurately. Altered data that are left uncorrected are unusable.
  • Timeliness: The system must deliver data in a timely manner (real-time data delivery without significant delay). For audio and video, late data is useless.
  • Jitter: Jitter refers to the variation in packet arrival time. It is the uneven delay in the delivery of audio or video packets that can cause jerky reception.

3. Five Components of Data Communication

  1. Message: The information (data) to be communicated (text, numbers, pictures, audio, video).
  2. Sender: The device that creates and sends the message (computer, workstation, telephone handset, video camera).
  3. Receiver: The device that receives the message (computer, workstation, TV, telephone).
  4. Transmission Medium: The physical path by which a message travels from sender to receiver (twisted-pair wire, coaxial cable, fiber-optic cable, radio waves).
  5. Protocol: A set of rules that governs data communications. It represents an agreement between the communicating devices. Without a protocol, two devices may be connected but cannot communicate.

Enumerate the functions of each layer of TCP/IP protocol suite with its layered diagram. What are the different types of addressing used at each layer?

Model Exam Answer

1. TCP/IP Architecture & Layer Functions

  • Application Layer: Provides network services directly to user applications. Deals with high-level protocols such as HTTP, SMTP, FTP, and DNS.
  • Transport Layer: Responsible for end-to-end process-to-process communication. Performs segmentation, flow control, connection management, error checking, and multiplexing/demultiplexing via port numbers. TCP provides reliable connection-oriented service; UDP provides lightweight connectionless service.
  • Network Layer: Responsible for host-to-host packet delivery across multiple interconnected networks. Handles logical addressing (IPv4/IPv6), routing (determining path via routing algorithms), and packet forwarding.
  • Data Link Layer: Responsible for node-to-node (hop-to-hop) frame delivery across a single physical network segment. Performs framing, hardware physical addressing (MAC addresses), error detection (CRC), flow control, and medium access control (MAC).
  • Physical Layer: Transmits individual raw bits over a physical transmission medium. Defines mechanical, electrical, and functional interfaces (voltage levels, data rates, bit duration, connectors).

What is transmission impairment? Briefly explain its causes with necessary diagrams.

Model Exam Answer

1. Definition of Transmission Impairment

Signals travel through transmission media, which are not perfect. The imperfection causes signal impairment. This means that the signal at the beginning of the medium is not identical to the signal at the end of the medium (what is sent is not what is received).

2. Three Causes of Transmission Impairment

  1. Attenuation (Loss of Energy): When a signal travels through a medium, it loses energy overcoming the resistance of the medium. To compensate for this loss, amplifiers are used to amplify the signal.
    Measurement in Decibels: $\text{dB} = 10 \log_{10} \left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)$ where $P_1$ is input power and $P_2$ is output power.
  2. Distortion (Alteration of Waveform): Occurs in composite signals made of different frequencies. Each frequency component has its own propagation speed through a medium and therefore arrives with a different phase delay at the receiver. Consequently, the components combine into a distorted shape.
  3. Noise: External electrical signals corrupting the original signal. Types include:
    • Thermal Noise: Random motion of electrons in a conductor.
    • Induced Noise: Comes from sources like motors and appliances (transmitters act as sending antennas and media act as receiving antennas).
    • Crosstalk: Effect of one wire on another (leakage between twisted pairs).
    • Impulse Noise: Spikes caused by lightning, power lines, or sparks.

    Signal-to-Noise Ratio: $\text{SNR} = \frac{\text{Signal Power}}{\text{Noise Power}}$, $\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 10 \log_{10}(\text{SNR})$.

We have a channel with a 2-MHz bandwidth. The SNR for this channel is 63. What are the appropriate bit rate and signal level? (To calculate signal level, consider the bit rate to be 4 Mbps less than the upper limit).

Model Exam Answer

Step-by-Step Numerical Solution

Given Parameters:

  • Bandwidth $B = 2\text{ MHz} = 2 \times 10^6\text{ Hz}$
  • Signal-to-Noise Ratio $\text{SNR} = 63$

Step 1: Calculate Theoretical Upper Limit (Shannon Capacity)

By Shannon's Capacity Formula:

$$\text{Capacity } C = B \log_2 (1 + \text{SNR})$$$$C = (2 \times 10^6) \times \log_2 (1 + 63) = (2 \times 10^6) \times \log_2 (64)$$$$\text{Since } 64 = 2^6 \implies \log_2(64) = 6$$$$C = 2 \times 10^6 \times 6 = 12 \times 10^6\text{ bps} = 12\text{ Mbps}$$

The upper limit of data rate is 12 Mbps.

Step 2: Determine Operating Bit Rate

According to the problem statement, the appropriate bit rate must be 4 Mbps less than the upper limit:

$$\text{Bit Rate } R = C - 4\text{ Mbps} = 12\text{ Mbps} - 4\text{ Mbps} = 8\text{ Mbps} = 8 \times 10^6\text{ bps}$$

Step 3: Determine Required Signal Level ($M$) using Nyquist Bit Rate Formula

By Nyquist's Bit Rate Theorem for a noiseless channel operating at this rate:

$$R = 2 \times B \times \log_2(M)$$$$8 \times 10^6 = 2 \times (2 \times 10^6) \times \log_2(M)$$$$8 \times 10^6 = 4 \times 10^6 \times \log_2(M)$$$$\log_2(M) = \frac{8 \times 10^6}{4 \times 10^6} = 2$$$$M = 2^2 = 4\text{ signal levels}$$

Final Result:

• Appropriate Bit Rate: 8 Mbps
• Required Signal Levels: 4 signal levels

Convert the digital data 11001010 to a digital signal by applying AMI and Differential Manchester line coding schemes.

Model Exam Answer

Bit Sequence: 1 1 0 0 1 0 1 0

1. Alternate Mark Inversion (AMI) Scheme

  • Rule: Binary 0 is represented by zero voltage ($0\text{ V}$). Binary 1s are represented by alternating positive ($+V$) and negative ($-V$) voltages.
  • Bit-by-Bit Encoding:
    • 1st bit = 1 $\rightarrow$ $+V$
    • 2nd bit = 1 $\rightarrow$ $-V$ (alternate)
    • 3rd bit = 0 $\rightarrow$ $0\text{ V}$
    • 4th bit = 0 $\rightarrow$ $0\text{ V}$
    • 5th bit = 1 $\rightarrow$ $+V$ (alternate)
    • 6th bit = 0 $\rightarrow$ $0\text{ V}$
    • 7th bit = 1 $\rightarrow$ $-V$ (alternate)
    • 8th bit = 0 $\rightarrow$ $0\text{ V}$
  • Waveform Levels: +V, -V, 0, 0, +V, 0, -V, 0

2. Differential Manchester Scheme

  • Rule: Always contains a mid-bit transition for synchronization. Bit 0 forces a transition at the beginning of the bit interval. Bit 1 has NO transition at the beginning of the bit interval.
  • Assuming initial state begins from High voltage:
    • Bit 1 (1): No transition at start; mid-bit transition High $\rightarrow$ Low.
    • Bit 2 (1): No transition at start; mid-bit transition Low $\rightarrow$ High.
    • Bit 3 (0): Transition at start (High $\rightarrow$ Low); mid-bit transition Low $\rightarrow$ High.
    • Bit 4 (0): Transition at start (High $\rightarrow$ Low); mid-bit transition Low $\rightarrow$ High.
    • Bit 5 (1): No transition at start; mid-bit transition High $\rightarrow$ Low.
    • Bit 6 (0): Transition at start (Low $\rightarrow$ High); mid-bit transition High $\rightarrow$ Low.
    • Bit 7 (1): No transition at start; mid-bit transition Low $\rightarrow$ High.
    • Bit 8 (0): Transition at start (High $\rightarrow$ Low); mid-bit transition Low $\rightarrow$ High.

Compare bit stuffing and byte stuffing with suitable examples.

Model Exam Answer

1. Byte Stuffing (Character-Oriented Framing)

In byte stuffing, a special 1-byte flag character (e.g., FLAG = 01111110) is added at the start and end of the frame. If the flag or escape byte appears in the payload text, a special escape byte (ESC = 00011011) is inserted before it.

  • Example: Original payload text: A FLAG B ESC C
  • Transmitted Stuffed Payload: FLAG A ESC FLAG B ESC ESC C FLAG
  • Receiver Processing: Whenever ESC is seen, the receiver strips ESC and retains the following byte as pure data.

2. Bit Stuffing (Bit-Oriented Framing)

In bit stuffing, frames begin and end with the unique 8-bit flag sequence 01111110 (six consecutive 1s). To guarantee that the flag pattern never occurs accidentally in data, the sender automatically injects a special 0 bit after every sequence of five consecutive 1s, regardless of the next bit value.

  • Example: Original bitstream: 0001111111001111101
  • After Bit Stuffing: 000111110110011111001 (underlined 0s inserted).
  • Receiver Processing: When the receiver sees five 1s followed by a 0, it strips the 0. If it sees five 1s followed by 10, it detects the true end-of-frame flag!

2. Field Explanations:

  • Hardware Type (16-bit): Defines physical network type (e.g., Ethernet = 1).

  3. Cache Storage: Host A receives the reply, updates its ARP table (TTL cache), and immediately delivers the buffered IP datagram.

Define cyclic codes and linear block codes. Given the dataword 101011 and the divisor 10011, generate the codeword using CRC polynomial division.

Model Exam Answer

1. Definitions

  • Linear Block Codes: A code is linear if the modulo-2 sum (XOR) of any two valid codewords is also a valid codeword in the code. Block codes divide message data into fixed $k$-bit datawords and append $(n-k)$ parity bits to produce $n$-bit codewords.
  • Cyclic Codes: A special subset of linear block codes where cyclically shifting any valid codeword produces another valid codeword. Implemented efficiently in hardware using feedback shift registers and modulo-2 polynomial division.

2. CRC Generation Problem

Given:

  • Dataword $D = 101011$ ($k = 6$ bits)
  • Divisor $G = 10011$ ($n - k + 1 = 5$ bits $\implies$ polynomial degree $r = 4$)

Step 1: Append $r = 4$ zeros to the dataword

$$\text{Augmented Dataword } = 101011\mathbf{0000}$$$$(10\text{ bits total})$$

Step 2: Modulo-2 Polynomial Division (XOR Division)

Dividend: 1010110000 | Divisor: 10011
1010110000 XOR 1001100000 -> 0011010000
0011010000 XOR 0010011000 -> 0001001000
0001001000 XOR 0001001100 -> 0000000100
Final Remainder (4 bits) = 0011

Quotient $= 101101$, Remainder $R = 0011$.

Step 3: Construct Codeword

$$\text{Codeword} = \text{Dataword} \cup \text{Remainder} = 101011\mathbf{0011}$$

• Transmitted CRC Codeword: 1010110011

Calculate the Internet checksum for the text 'ISE DEPARTMENT' at sender and receiver side (assume hexadecimal ASCII: I=49, S=53, E=45, space=32, D=44, P=50, A=41, R=52, T=54, M=4D, N=4E).

Model Exam Answer

1. ASCII to 16-Bit Word Mapping

The text is 14 characters: "ISE DEPARTMENT"

  1. "IS" = 4953 (hex)
  2. "E " = 4520 (hex) (E = 45, space = 32 in hex = 20)
  3. "DE" = 4445 (hex)
  4. "PA" = 5041 (hex)
  5. "RT" = 5254 (hex)
  6. "ME" = 4D45 (hex)
  7. "NT" = 4E54 (hex)

2. Sender Side 16-Bit One's Complement Addition

$$\begin{aligned}4953 + 4520 &= 8E73 \\8E73 + 4445 &= D2B8 \\D2B8 + 5041 &= 122F9 \rightarrow (22F9 + 1) = 22FA \text{ (wrap carry)} \\22FA + 5254 &= 754E \\754E + 4D45 &= C293 \\C293 + 4E54 &= 110E7 \rightarrow (10E7 + 1) = 10E8 \text{ (wrap carry)}\end{aligned}$$$$\text{Sum} = 10E8_{16}$$$$\text{Checksum (One's complement of Sum)} = \sim(10E8) = EF17_{16}$$

The sender transmits the data words along with the Checksum EF17.

The receiver sums all 7 data words plus the received checksum:

$$\text{Sum of data words} = 10E8_{16}$$$$\text{Total Sum} = 10E8_{16} + EF17_{16} = FFFF_{16}$$$$\text{Receiver Checksum} = \sim(FFFF) = 0000_{16}$$

Briefly discuss the working of Stop-and-Wait protocol for a noisy channel (Stop-and-Wait ARQ) with necessary flow diagrams.

Model Exam Answer

1. Working of Stop-and-Wait ARQ

Stop-and-Wait Automatic Repeat reQuest (ARQ) adds error control and flow control mechanisms to simple Stop-and-Wait over noisy channels:

  • Sequence Numbers: Frames alternate between sequence number 0 and 1 (using 1-bit sequence number $m=1, 2^1=2$).
  • Acknowledgment Numbers: ACKs announce the sequence number of the next expected frame (ACK 0 or ACK 1).
  • Retransmission Timer: The sender maintains a timer. If an ACK does not arrive before timer expiration (due to a lost frame or lost ACK), the sender retransmits the copy kept in its buffer.

2. Operation Scenarios:

  1. Normal Operation: Sender sends Frame 0 $\rightarrow$ Receiver receives Frame 0, sends ACK 1 $\rightarrow$ Sender sends Frame 1.
  2. Lost Frame: Sender sends Frame 0 $\rightarrow$ Frame lost in transit $\rightarrow$ Sender timer expires $\rightarrow$ Sender retransmits Frame 0 $\rightarrow$ Receiver receives Frame 0, sends ACK 1.
  3. Lost ACK: Sender sends Frame 0 $\rightarrow$ Receiver sends ACK 1, but ACK is lost $\rightarrow$ Sender timer expires $\rightarrow$ Sender retransmits Frame 0 $\rightarrow$ Receiver discards duplicate Frame 0 and re-sends ACK 1!

Consider m=2, and with necessary diagrams justify the statement: 'Size of the sender window must be equal to 2^(m-1) in Selective Repeat ARQ Protocol'.

Model Exam Answer

1. Theoretical Principle

In Selective Repeat ARQ, both sender window size $W_s$ and receiver window size $W_r$ must be equal, and satisfy:

$$W_s = W_r = 2^{m-1}$$

For $m = 2$ bits, sequence numbers are $0, 1, 2, 3$. The allowable window size is $W = 2^{2-1} = 2$.

2. Proof by Contradiction: What happens if $W > 2^{m-1}$ (e.g., $W = 3$)?

  1. Suppose $W = 3$ with sequence numbers $0, 1, 2, 3$. Sender window contains frames $[0, 1, 2]$.
  2. Sender transmits frames 0, 1, and 2.
  3. Receiver correctly receives frames 0, 1, 2 and advances its window to $[3, 0, 1]$.
  4. Receiver sends ACKs 0, 1, 2 back to the sender, but all ACKs are lost in transit!
  5. Sender's timer expires for Frame 0. Sender retransmits Frame 0.
  6. The receiver is currently waiting for window $[3, 0, 1]$. Because 0 is in the receiver's new window, the receiver mistakes the retransmitted duplicate Frame 0 for the brand new Frame 0 of the next cycle!
  7. This causes fatal undetected duplicate data acceptance.

3. Correct Case when $W = 2^{m-1} = 2$:

With $W = 2$, sender window is $[0, 1]$ and receiver window is $[0, 1]$. When receiver accepts frames 0 and 1, receiver window slides to $[2, 3]$. If all ACKs are lost and sender retransmits Frame 0, 0 is completely outside receiver's window $[2, 3]$. Receiver recognizes it as a duplicate and discards it! Hence, window size must be $\le 2^{m-1}$.

Justify the statement 'Size of the send window must be less than 2^m in Go-Back-N ARQ protocol' with suitable diagrams.

Model Exam Answer

1. Principle of Go-Back-N Window Sizing

In Go-Back-N ARQ with an $m$-bit sequence number ($2^m$ sequence numbers $0$ to $2^m - 1$), the maximum send window size must satisfy:

$$W_s \le 2^m - 1$$

2. Justification (What if $W_s = 2^m$?):

  • Let $m = 2$, so sequence numbers are $0, 1, 2, 3$ ($2^m = 4$). Suppose $W_s = 4$.
  • Sender transmits frames 0, 1, 2, 3.
  • Receiver receives all 4 frames in order and sends cumulative ACK 4 (meaning expecting Frame 0 of next cycle).
  • If ACK 4 is lost, sender times out and retransmits frames 0, 1, 2, 3.
  • The receiver is expecting Frame 0 of the next cycle. It cannot distinguish whether Frame 0 is a retransmitted duplicate or new data!
  • By limiting $W_s \le 2^m - 1 = 3$, the window covers at most $[0, 1, 2]$. When ACK is lost, retransmitted Frame 0 can never overlap with next cycle's expected frame.

A pure ALOHA network transmits 200-bit frames on a shared channel of 200 kbps. What is the throughput if the system produces: (i) 1000 frames/sec, (ii) 500 frames/sec, (iii) 250 frames/sec?

Model Exam Answer

1. Parameter Calculations

  • Frame length $L = 200\text{ bits}$
  • Channel bandwidth $R = 200\text{ kbps} = 200,000\text{ bps}$
  • Frame transmission time $T_{\text{fr}} = \frac{L}{R} = \frac{200}{200,000} = 1\text{ ms} = 0.001\text{ sec}$

Throughput formula for Pure ALOHA: $S = G e^{-2G}$, where $G = \text{load} = N \times T_{\text{fr}}$

2. Calculations for Each Load:

  1. Case (i): 1000 frames/sec
    $G = 1000 \times 0.001 = 1.0$
    $S = 1.0 \times e^{-2(1.0)} = 1.0 \times e^{-2} = 1.0 \times 0.1353 = 0.1353$ (13.53%)
    Throughput in bps $= S \times R = 0.1353 \times 200\text{ kbps} = 27.06\text{ kbps}$ (or $135.3\text{ frames/sec}$).
  2. Case (ii): 500 frames/sec
    $G = 500 \times 0.001 = 0.5$
    $S = 0.5 \times e^{-2(0.5)} = 0.5 \times e^{-1} = 0.5 \times 0.3679 = 0.1839$ (18.39% — peak capacity!)
    Throughput in bps $= 0.1839 \times 200\text{ kbps} = 36.79\text{ kbps}$ (or $183.9\text{ frames/sec}$).
  3. Case (iii): 250 frames/sec
    $G = 250 \times 0.001 = 0.25$
    $S = 0.25 \times e^{-2(0.25)} = 0.25 \times e^{-0.5} = 0.25 \times 0.6065 = 0.1516$ (15.16%)
    Throughput in bps $= 0.1516 \times 200\text{ kbps} = 30.33\text{ kbps}$ (or $151.6\text{ frames/sec}$).

What is the purpose of a subnet mask in IPv4 addressing? How is it used to determine the network and host portion of an IP address?

Model Exam Answer

1. Purpose of a Subnet Mask

A subnet mask is a 32-bit binary number that demarcates which part of an IPv4 address corresponds to the network prefix and which part corresponds to the host identifier. In dotted-decimal notation, 1s indicate network bits and 0s indicate host bits (e.g., 255.255.255.0 has 24 ones and 8 zeros, written as /24).

2. Bitwise Operation to Extract Network Address

To determine the network address, the router performs a bitwise logical AND operation between the destination IP address and the subnet mask:

$$\text{Network Address} = \text{IP Address} \text{ AND } \text{Subnet Mask}$$
  • Example: Given IP address 192.168.10.75 and mask 255.255.255.192 (/26):
    • IP in binary: 11000000.10101000.00001010.01001011
    • Mask in binary: 11000000.11111111.11111111.11000000
    • Bitwise AND: 11000000.10101000.00001010.01000000 $\implies$ 192.168.10.64
  • The remaining 6 bits identify host number $11_2 = 11$ within the subnet 192.168.10.64/26.

What are the restrictions imposed on classless address blocks? An Organization is granted the block 16.14.80.0/20. Divide this block into four equal subnets and find the subnet mask, first host, last host, and broadcast address for each subnet.

Model Exam Answer

1. Three Restrictions on CIDR Address Blocks

  1. Contiguity: All addresses in a block must be contiguous (consecutive numbers).
  2. Power of 2: The number of addresses in a block must be a power of $2$ ($2^{32-n}$).
  3. Divisibility: The first address in the block must be evenly divisible by the total number of addresses in the block.

Define Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP) in networking. What does it physically represent?

Model Exam Answer

1. Definition and Formula

The Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP) is the product of a link's transmission bandwidth (in bits per second) and its one-way propagation delay (in seconds):

$$\text{BDP} = \text{Bandwidth (bps)} \times \text{Propagation Delay (seconds)}$$

2. Physical Interpretation: 'Volume of the Pipe'

  • BDP defines the maximum number of bits that can fill the transmission medium at any given instant.
  • Think of the link as a pipe carrying bits. Bandwidth is the cross-sectional area of the pipe, and delay is the length of the pipe. BDP is the total volume of the pipe in bits.
  • Importance: If a sender wishes to achieve 100% link utilization without stalling, its sender window size must be at least equal to $2 \times \text{BDP}$ (round-trip bandwidth-delay product).
