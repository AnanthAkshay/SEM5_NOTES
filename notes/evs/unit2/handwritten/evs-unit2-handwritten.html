1. Natural Resources & Sustainability

Definition

A Natural Resource is any raw material, physical matter, or energy flow provided by the Earth that is essential or useful for sustaining human civilization and ecological integrity.

Classification Definition & Replenishment Rate Core Examples
Renewable Resources (Inexhaustible / Regenerative) Resources replenished naturally through biogeochemical or physical cycles at a rate equal to or faster than consumption. Solar radiation, wind kinetic energy, tidal flow, biomass (when harvested below maximum sustainable yield), freshwater.
Non-Renewable Resources (Exhaustible) Finite geological reserves formed over millions of years that cannot regenerate on human timescales once extracted. Fossil fuels (coal, petroleum, natural gas), metallic ores (iron, bauxite, copper), uranium, non-metallic minerals.
Quick Recall

Renewable = regenerated by natural cycles (solar, wind, water). Non-renewable = finite geological reserves (fossil fuels, metallic minerals).

2. Forest Resources & Deforestation

Forests are Earth's lungs, covering approximately 31%31\%31% of global land area. They serve critical economic functions (timber, pulp, resin, medicinal herbs) and invaluable ecological services (carbon sequestration, oxygen generation, hydrological regulation, soil stabilization).

2.1 Deforestation: Causes & Ecological Fallout

  • Principal Causes: Agricultural expansion (slash-and-burn / shifting cultivation), commercial timber logging, open-cast mining in forested belts, large-scale hydroelectric reservoirs, urban infrastructure corridors.
  • Consequences: Accelerated soil erosion, catastrophic loss of biodiversity, disruption of regional microclimate and rainfall cycles, increased atmospheric CO2CO_2CO2​, and displacement of indigenous forest communities.

2.2 Dams & Forest Conservation Movements

  • Dams & Tribal Displacement: Mega-dam projects submerge thousands of hectares of prime virgin forest (e.g. Sardar Sarovar, Tehri Dam) and forcibly displace indigenous tribal populations without adequate rehabilitation.
  • The Chipko Movement (1973, Uttarakhand): Led by Sunderlal Bahuguna and Chandi Prasad Bhatt; villagers (notably rural women) hugged trees to prevent commercial loggers from cutting them down.
  • The Appiko Movement (1983, Western Ghats, Karnataka): Inspired by Chipko, villagers in Uttara Kannada embraced forest trees with the slogan "Ubsu, Belasu, Balasu" (Save, Grow, and Wisely Use).
Quick Recall

Deforestation causes soil loss, climate shifts, and tribal displacement. Grassroots conservation: Chipko (Uttarakhand, Sunderlal Bahuguna) and Appiko (Karnataka Western Ghats).

3. Water Resources & Rainwater Harvesting

Freshwater constitutes only 2.5%2.5\%2.5% of global water reserves. Human extraction for agriculture (70%70\%70%), industry (20%20\%20%), and municipal use (10%10\%10%) is creating severe global water scarcity.

3.1 Over-utilization & Groundwater Depletion

  • Over-pumping: Deep tube-wells extract groundwater faster than monsoon recharge rates, plunging water tables downward by meters per year in agricultural breadbaskets (Punjab, Karnataka).
  • Land Subsidence & Contamination: Excessive pumping causes aquifer compaction, coastal saltwater intrusion, and mobilization of toxic geogenic fluoride and arsenic into drinking supplies.
  • Inter-State River Conflicts: Severe geopolitical disputes over shared river basins during dry spells (e.g. the Cauvery River Dispute between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, and the Krishna Water Dispute).

3.2 Rainwater Harvesting (RWH)

Rainwater harvesting captures and stores rainwater for direct use or for recharging subterranean aquifers via recharge pits and borewells.

Formula: Annual Rooftop Rainwater Harvest Potential

4. Mineral Resources & Mining Impacts

Definition

A Mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid substance possessing a definite chemical composition and an ordered internal crystalline atomic structure.

4.1 Environmental Fallout of Mining

  • Landscape Destruction: Open-cast surface mining strips overburden vegetation, creating massive craters and mine dumps.
  • Acid Mine Drainage (AMD): When sulfide minerals (e.g. pyrite, FeS2FeS_2FeS2​) are exposed to air and water during mining, bacterial oxidation generates sulfuric acid (H2SO4H_2SO_4H2​SO4​), which leaches toxic heavy metals into streams: 4FeS2+15O2+14H2O⟶4Fe(OH)3↓+8H2SO44FeS_2 + 15O_2 + 14H_2O \longrightarrow 4Fe(OH)_3 \downarrow + 8H_2SO_44FeS2​+15O2​+14H2​O⟶4Fe(OH)3​↓+8H2​SO4​
  • Air & Noise Pollution: Blasting, drilling, and hauling generate fugitive particulate matter (PM2.5,PM10PM_{2.5}, PM_{10}PM2.5​,PM10​) causing silicosis in miners.
  • Tailings Dam Failure: Collapse of wet tailing ponds releases millions of tons of toxic slimes into river networks.

Minerals are naturally occurring inorganic solids. Mining causes land degradation, heavy metal toxicity, and Acid Mine Drainage (sulfide oxidation generating H2SO4H_2SO_4H2​SO4​).

6. Land Resources & Soil Erosion

Soil is a non-renewable resource on human timescales, requiring centuries to form a single centimeter of topsoil through bedrock weathering.

6.1 Mechanisms & Types of Soil Erosion

A. Wind Erosion (Exam Focus: CIE 1 2024 Question):

  • 1. Saltation (50−70%50-70\%50−70% of wind erosion): Fine-to-medium sand particles (0.1−0.5 mm0.1 - 0.5\text{ mm}0.1−0.5 mm) bounce along the surface in short hops, dislodging other particles upon impact.
  • 2. Surface Creep (5−25%5-25\%5−25% of wind erosion): Larger particles (0.5−2.0 mm0.5 - 2.0\text{ mm}0.5−2.0 mm) too heavy to be lifted are rolled or pushed along the ground by colliding saltating grains.
  • 3. Suspension (3−15%3-15\%3−15% of wind erosion): Extremely fine silt and dust particles (<0.1 mm< 0.1\text{ mm}<0.1 mm) are lifted high into the atmosphere and transported thousands of kilometers as dust storms.

B. Water Erosion:

  • Sheet Erosion: Uniform detachment and thin horizontal removal of topsoil by overland sheet runoff.
  • Rill Erosion: Concentrated runoff cuts small, shallow, distinct finger-like channels (rills).
  • Gully Erosion: Rills converge into deep, steep-sided channels (gullies) that dissect croplands into uncultivable ravines (badland topography).

6.2 Soil Conservation Measures

  • Contour Bunding & Terracing: Sculpting steep slopes into stepped terraces to slow surface runoff velocity.
  • Afforestation & Shelterbelts: Planting dense tree windbreaks perpendicular to prevailing winds to block wind erosion.
  • Mulching & Cover Cropping: Retaining crop residues on soil to prevent direct raindrop impact detachment.
Quick Recall

Wind erosion types: Saltation (bouncing grains, 50-70%), Surface creep (rolling coarse grains), Suspension (fine dust airborne). Water erosion: Sheet → Rill → Gully.

7. Energy Resources (Fossil Fuels vs. Renewable Alternatives)

7.1 Environmental Fallout of Fossil Fuels

  • Coal Combustion: Emits large amounts of CO2CO_2CO2​ (greenhouse gas driving global warming), SO2SO_2SO2​ and NOxNO_xNOx​ (causing Acid Rain, pH<5.6pH < 5.6pH<5.6), particulate matter, and toxic fly ash containing heavy metals (mercury, lead).
  • Petroleum / Transportation: Major source of photochemical smog (ground-level ozone, PANPANPAN), carbon monoxide (COCOCO), and marine oil spills.
  • Nuclear Power: Zero greenhouse gas emissions during operation, but carries catastrophic risk of core meltdown (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011) and unsolved challenges of long-term deep geological disposal of high-level radioactive waste (plutonium isotopes with half-lives exceeding 24,00024,00024,000 years).

7.2 Renewable Energy Alternatives

  • Solar Energy: Photovoltaic (PV) cells convert sunlight directly into electricity via silicon semiconductor p-n junctions; solar thermal collectors generate heat/steam.
  • Wind Energy: Aerodynamic wind turbine blades capture atmospheric kinetic energy; clean, modular, zero operational water use.
  • Biomass & Biogas: Anaerobic digestion of animal manure and organic waste by methanogenic bacteria produces Biogas (55−70% CH4,30−45% CO255-70\% \text{ CH}_4, 30-45\% \text{ CO}_255−70% CH4​,30−45% CO2​), providing clean domestic cooking fuel while yielding nitrogen-rich organic slurry fertilizer.
  • Hydroelectric & Geothermal: Hydro harness gravitational potential of flowing water; Geothermal taps subterranean hydrothermal steam.
Quick Recall

Fossil fuels drive acid rain (SO2,NOxSO_2, NO_xSO2​,NOx​) and global warming (CO2CO_2CO2​). Renewables (Solar PV, Wind, Biogas [methane]) provide decentralized, low-carbon power.

📝 Practice Problem:
CIE-1 Q2.b [5M] Explain the effects of overuse of fresh water and problems related to water resources.
Ans

Official Model Solution: Over-exploitation of freshwater leads to critical hydrological crises:

  1. Water Table Depletion: Intensive tube-well extraction for green-revolution agriculture exceeds natural monsoon recharge, causing regional aquifer water tables to fall by meters annually.
  2. Saltwater Intrusion: Excessive drawdown in coastal areas reverses hydraulic gradients, pulling dense saline seawater into freshwater municipal aquifers and ruining drinking water supplies.
  3. Land Subsidence: Over-extraction drains interstitial pore water from deep silt/clay strata, causing the overburden ground to compact and sink irreversibly (e.g., Mexico City, Jakarta, Venice).
  4. Waterlogging and Soil Salinization: In canal-irrigated zones, excessive irrigation without adequate drainage raises the water table into the root zone, causing capillary action to evaporate water and leave a toxic crust of alkaline salts on arable soils.
  5. Riparian & Interstate Water Conflicts: Upstream impoundments restrict downstream flows during dry seasons, causing severe socio-economic disputes (e.g. Cauvery River dispute between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu).

PRACTICE 4 • WATERLOGGING VS SALINIZATION MECHANISMS

Differentiate between waterlogging and soil salinization in agricultural land management.

📝 Practice Problem:
Show Step-by-Step Solution
Ans
  • Waterlogging: Physical condition where excessive irrigation water saturates soil pore spaces, pushing the water table into the plant root zone, displacing oxygen, and causing anaerobic root asphyxiation.
  • Soil Salinization: Chemical degradation where high water tables evaporate in arid/semi-arid climates, drawing dissolved minerals upward via capillary action and depositing a sterile white crust of alkaline sodium, calcium, and magnesium salts on topsoil.

PRACTICE 6 • ECOLOGICAL CONFLICTS OF MEGA DAMS

Analyze the key environmental and social trade-offs of large river valley projects with reference to Sardar Sarovar and Tehri Dams.

📝 Practice Problem:
Show Step-by-Step Solution
Ans

While multipurpose mega dams generate hydroelectricity and irrigation water, they incur severe ecological and socio-economic trade-offs:

  • Forest & Biodiversity Submergence: Inundation of vast tracts of pristine riverine forests, destroying wildlife corridors and endemic habitats.
  • Tribal Displacement & Resettlement Trauma: Uprooting indigenous tribal communities from ancestral lands without adequate rehabilitation (the core focus of Medha Patkar's Narmada Bachao Andolan).
  • Reservoir-Induced Seismicity (RIS): Massive concentrated water masses in seismic zones (e.g. Tehri Dam in the Central Himalayan seismic gap) create pore pressure lubrication along active fault lines.
  • Silt Trap & Downstream Delta Erosion: Trapping fertile nutrient silts in reservoirs deprives downstream estuaries, starving agricultural deltas and triggering coastal erosion.
