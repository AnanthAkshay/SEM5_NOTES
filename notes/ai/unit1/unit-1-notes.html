§ 1

What is AI? The Four Historical Approaches

Historically, researchers have approached Artificial Intelligence along two fundamental dimensions: whether the goal is to match human performance or adhere to an ideal standard of rationality, and whether the focus is on internal thought processes or external behavior (actions).

Focus Dimension Human-Centric Approach Rationality-Centric (Ideal Standard)
Thinking (Mind / Reasoning) 1. Thinking Humanly (Cognitive Modeling)
Requires determining how humans think through psychological experiments, brain scans (fMRI), or introspective analysis. Compares computer traces to human cognitive behavior. 		2. Thinking Rationally (Laws of Thought)
Rooted in Aristotle's syllogisms and formal mathematical logic. Focuses on irrefutable reasoning patterns ($P \implies Q, P \vdash Q$). Limited by computational tractability and expressing informal uncertain knowledge.
Acting (Behavior / Output) 3. Acting Humanly (The Turing Test)
Proposed by Alan Turing (1950). An agent passes if a human interrogator cannot distinguish its conversational replies from those of a human. Requires NLP, knowledge representation, automated reasoning, and machine learning. 		4. Acting Rationally (The Rational Agent) — MODERN STANDARD
A rational agent is one that acts so as to achieve the best expected outcome, or best expected utility when uncertainty is present. Correct inference is one way to act rationally, but reflexive safety actions are also rational.
WHY MODERN AI FOCUSES ON THE RATIONAL AGENT APPROACH

The rational agent approach is superior to human modeling for two main reasons:

  1. It is more general than the "laws of thought" approach, because correct inference is only one of several mechanisms for achieving rationality (e.g. reflex actions can be rational without logical deduction).
  2. It is more mathematically amenable to scientific development than approaches based on human behavior, because the standard of rationality is mathematically well-defined and completely independent of human biological quirks and cognitive errors.
QUICK RECALL
  • 4 Approaches: Thinking Humanly, Acting Humanly (Turing), Thinking Rationally (Logic), Acting Rationally (Agents).
  • Turing Test requires: NLP, Knowledge Representation, Automated Reasoning, Machine Learning. (Total Turing Test adds Computer Vision & Robotics).
  • Modern AI standard: Acting Rationally (maximizing expected performance).
Source: Russell & Norvig 4e, Ch. 1.1, pp. 2–4; Turing (1950); slides 16–22
§ 2

Intelligent Agents and Environments

An agent is anything that can be viewed as perceiving its environment through sensors and acting upon that environment through actuators.

AGENT MATHEMATICAL FORMALISM
  • Percept: The agent's perceptual inputs at any given instant.
  • Percept Sequence: The complete history of everything the agent has ever perceived: $P^*$.
  • Agent Function: An abstract mathematical mapping from any percept sequence to an action: $$f: P^* \to A$$
  • Agent Program: The concrete computational implementation of the agent function running on a physical computing architecture: $$\text{Agent} = \text{Architecture} + \text{Program}$$
Agent Interaction with its Environment
QUICK RECALL
  • Agent perceives environment via sensors; affects environment via actuators.
  • Agent Function is an abstract mathematical mapping: $f: P^* \to A$.
  • Agent Program is the concrete software code running on the physical architecture.
Source: Russell & Norvig 4e, Ch. 1.2, pp. 5–16; slides 23–30
§ 3

The Concept of Rationality and Autonomy

What is rational at any given time depends on four factors: the performance measure that defines the criterion of success, the agent's prior knowledge of the environment, the actions that the agent can perform, and the agent's percept sequence to date.

DEFINITION OF A RATIONAL AGENT

For each possible percept sequence, a rational agent should select an action that is expected to maximize its performance measure, given the evidence provided by the percept sequence and whatever built-in knowledge the agent has.

Critical Distinctions in Rationality

Concept Key Definition Crucial Exam Contrast
Omniscience vs Rationality An omniscient agent knows the actual outcome of its actions and can act accordingly. Omniscience is impossible in reality. Rationality maximizes expected performance based on available information, not actual outcome (e.g. looking both ways before crossing a street is rational, even if a meteorite strikes).
Information Gathering & Exploration Doing actions to modify future percepts (e.g. looking around, medical tests). A rational agent must gather information and explore unfamiliar environments rather than blindly acting on initial assumptions.
Autonomy An agent is autonomous to the extent that its behavior is determined by its own experience (learning), rather than solely by the initial knowledge programmed by its designer. A non-autonomous agent relies entirely on pre-baked assumptions and fails when the environment changes. Truly rational agents learn to become autonomous over time.
QUICK RECALL
  • Rationality depends on: Performance measure, Prior knowledge, Actions, Percept sequence.
  • Rationality $\ne$ Omniscience. Rationality maximizes expected success, not perfection in hindsight.
  • Autonomy = learning from experience rather than relying solely on built-in prior knowledge.
Source: Russell & Norvig 4e, Ch. 1.3, pp. 16–28; slides 31–45

Environment Classifications & Dimensions

The design of an agent program is determined almost entirely by the nature of its task environment. Environments are classified across seven fundamental conceptual dimensions.

Dimension Dichotomy Definition Example: Easy vs Hard
Observability Fully Observable: Agent's sensors give complete access to the entire state of the environment at all times.
Partially Observable: Noise, inaccurate sensors, or hidden state prevents full view.		 Chess is fully observable.
Poker / Driving is partially observable.
Number of Agents Single-Agent: Agent operates alone.
Multi-Agent: Environment contains multiple agents. Can be Competitive (adversarial) or Cooperative.		 Crossword puzzle is single-agent.
Chess is competitive multi-agent.
Determinism Deterministic: Next state is completely determined by current state and the agent's action.
Stochastic: Uncertainty or randomness (probabilities) affect outcomes.		 Vacuum world without slippage is deterministic.
Autonomous driving is stochastic.
Episodic vs Sequential Episodic: Experience is divided into atomic episodes; current decision does not affect future episodes.
Sequential: Current action affects all future states and rewards (requires planning).		 Defective part detection is episodic.
Chess and Maze navigation are sequential.
Static vs Dynamic Static: Environment does not change while the agent is deliberating.
Dynamic: Environment continuously changes while the agent thinks (semidynamic = score drops).		 Crossword is static.
Taxi driving is dynamic.
Timed chess is semidynamic.
Discrete vs Continuous Discrete: Finite, distinct number of states, actions, and percepts.
Continuous: Smoothly varying values over time (speed, coordinates).		 Chess is discrete.
Taxi driving is continuous.
Known vs Unknown Known: The laws / physics of the environment are known to the agent.
Unknown: The agent must explore and learn how the environment works.		 Video game with known rules is known.
Real world exploration is unknown.
EXAM SUMMARY: THE HARDEST ENVIRONMENT TYPE

The most difficult task environment for an AI agent is: Partially observable, Multi-agent, Stochastic, Sequential, Dynamic, Continuous, and Unknown (e.g. Automated Taxi Driving in a foreign city!).

  • Chess: Fully observable, Deterministic, Sequential, Static, Discrete, Known.

§ 6

Internal Agent Architectures

Russell and Norvig define five basic agent architectures of increasing sophistication: Simple Reflex, Model-Based Reflex, Goal-Based, Utility-Based, and Learning Agents.

Agent Architecture Internal State & Mechanism Action Selection Rule Limitation / Failure Mode
1. Simple Reflex Agent No memory of past percepts. Direct link from current percept to action. Condition-Action Rules:
if condition then action		 Infinite loops in partially observable environments (e.g. can't escape if state looks identical).
2. Model-Based Reflex Agent Maintains an internal state tracking aspects of the unobserved world. Updates state using transition model ("How world evolves") and sensor model ("How actions affect world"). Lacks explicit future goals; only acts on current beliefs.
3. Goal-Based Agent Combines internal state with explicit Goal information describing desirable situations. Uses Search and Planning to find sequences of actions that achieve the goal. Binary satisfaction (goal achieved vs not); cannot trade off between conflicting goals.
4. Utility-Based Agent Uses an explicit Utility function mapping states to real numbers (degrees of happiness). Selects the action that maximizes Expected Utility. Computationally expensive; designing complex multi-attribute utility functions is hard.
5. Learning Agent Divided into 4 functional modules: Critic, Learning Element, Performance Element, Problem Generator. Learns from feedback to improve performance beyond initial programming. Requires training samples and exploration-exploitation balance.

The Four Components of a Learning Agent

1
Critic: Evaluates how well the agent is doing against an external, fixed performance standard. Provides feedback to the learning element.
2
Learning Element: Responsible for making improvements. Takes critic feedback and experiments with new behaviors.
3
Performance Element: The traditional agent that selects external actions based on percepts (what we previously called the agent).
4
Problem Generator: Suggests actions that lead to new and informative experiences (exploration), even if they may yield suboptimal short-term rewards.
QUICK RECALL
  • Simple reflex: condition-action rules; fails if partially observable.
  • Model-based: internal state tracks the world.
  • Goal-based: uses search/planning to achieve goal.
  • Utility-based: maximizes expected utility (handles trade-offs).
  • Learning agent: Critic, Learning Element, Performance Element, Problem Generator.

§ 7

Problem Solving by Search & The 5 Components

A problem-solving agent is a goal-based agent that decides what to do by finding sequences of actions that lead to desirable states. When the correct action cannot be determined immediately, the agent formulates a search problem.

5 FIVE COMPONENTS OF A WELL-DEFINED SEARCH PROBLEM
  1. Initial State ($s_0$): The state that the agent starts in (e.g. $In(Arad)$).
  2. Actions ($Actions(s)$): A description of the possible actions available to the agent in state $s$. Given state $s$, $Actions(s)$ returns the set of legal actions.
  3. Transition Model ($Result(s, a)$): A description of what each action does. $Result(s, a)$ returns the state that results from doing action $a$ in state $s$.
  4. Goal Test ($IsGoal(s)$): Determines whether a given state $s$ is a goal state. Can be explicit (e.g. $s = In(Bucharest)$) or implicit (e.g. checkmate in chess).
  5. Path Cost ($c(s, a, s')$): A function that assigns a numeric cost to each step. The step cost of taking action $a$ in state $s$ to reach $s'$ is denoted $c(s, a, s')$. The total path cost is the sum of step costs along the path.

A solution is an action sequence that leads from the initial state to a goal state. An optimal solution is a solution that has the lowest path cost among all solutions.

QUICK RECALL
  • 5 Components: Initial State, Actions, Transition Model, Goal Test, Path Cost.
  • State Space: set of all reachable states from the initial state.
  • Optimal solution = lowest path cost among all solutions.
Source: Russell & Norvig 4e, Ch. 2.4, pp. 49–58; slides 36–55

State Space Formulations for Toy & Real-World Problems

A state space is the complete set of all states reachable from the initial state by any sequence of actions. State space formulation abstracts away irrelevant physical details to focus on formal problem solving.

CORE PROBLEM FORMULATION • VERIFIED SCRIPT PASS [10 MARKS • EXAM CORE]

Formal State-Space Formulation of the 8-Puzzle

The 8-puzzle consists of a $3 \times 3$ board with 8 numbered sliding tiles and one blank space.

1. Formal 5-Component Specification:

  • States: A specification of the position of each of the 8 numbered tiles and the blank square in the $3 \times 3$ grid.
  • Initial State: Any valid configuration of the board.
  • Actions: Movements of the blank space: $\{\text{Left}, \text{Right}, \text{Up}, \text{Down}\}$. (Moving the blank is cleaner than moving individual tiles!).
  • Transition Model: Given state $s$ and blank move $a$, returns the resulting board with the blank and adjacent tile swapped.
  • Goal Test: Matches the designated goal configuration (e.g. tiles ordered 1 to 8 with blank at bottom-right or center).
  • Path Cost: Each step costs 1 (path cost = number of moves).

2. State Space Size & Inversion Parity Analysis:

  • Total distinct arrangements of 9 squares $= 9! = \mathbf{362,880\text{ states}}$.
  • Inversion Parity Theorem: The state space is partitioned into two disjoint graph components of size $9! / 2 = 181,440$ states each. Any legal slide move preserves the even/odd parity of the number of tile inversions. Exactly half the states can reach the goal; the other half are provably impossible!

3. Branching Factor:

  • Blank in corner (4 corners): 2 legal moves.
  • Blank on edge (4 edges): 3 legal moves.
  • Blank in center (1 center): 4 legal moves.
  • Average branching factor: $b = \frac{4(2) + 4(3) + 1(4)}{9} = \frac{8 + 12 + 4}{9} = \frac{24}{9} \approx \mathbf{2.67}$.
FORMULATION 2 • 8-QUEENS PROBLEM [8 MARKS]

Incremental vs Complete-State Formulation of 8-Queens

Place 8 queens on an $8 \times 8$ chessboard such that no queen attacks another (no two queens share the same row, column, or diagonal).

Formulation Style State Representation State Space Size Search Methodology
Naive Incremental Any arrangement of 0 to 8 queens on the 64 squares. $$\binom{64}{8} \approx 4.4 \times 10^9\text{ states}$$ Place queen on any empty square. Massive redundant search.
Clever Incremental Place one queen per column in columns 1 to $k$ such that no queen is attacked. $$8^8 \approx 1.68 \times 10^7\text{ states}$$ Place next queen in leftmost empty column without conflict.
Complete-State Formulation All 8 queens are on the board, exactly one queen per column. $$8! = \mathbf{40,320\text{ states}}$$ Used by local search (Hill Climbing / Min-Conflicts) to slide queens vertically within their column.
QUICK RECALL
  • 8-puzzle: $9! = 362,880$ permutations; $181,440$ reachable states due to inversion parity.
  • 8-puzzle average branching factor $b = 24/9 \approx 2.67$.
  • 8-queens complete-state formulation reduces search space from $4.4 \times 10^9$ to $8! = 40,320$.

Dimension Human-Centric Approach Rationality-Centric (Ideal Standard)
Thinking 1. Thinking Humanly (Cognitive Modeling):

Ramaiah CIE-1 • October 29, 2025 • Question 2.a [4 Marks]

"Explain Model-Based Reflex Agent with a neat schematic diagram and algorithmic flow."

Show Complete Step-by-Step Model Answer

1. Architectural Purpose & Motivation:

A Simple Reflex Agent fails whenever the task environment is partially observable, because multiple distinct world states can generate identical sensory percepts, causing the agent to fall into infinite loops. A Model-Based Reflex Agent overcomes this limitation by maintaining an internal state that tracks aspects of the world currently unobservable by sensors.

2. Two Core Internal Knowledge Models:

  • Transition Model ("How the world evolves"): Knowledge of the physical dynamics of the environment independent of the agent (e.g., if a car is moving ahead, it will continue moving forward in the next time step).
  • Sensor / Effect Model ("What my actions do"): Knowledge of how the agent's own actions affect the state of the world (e.g., turning the steering wheel left causes the vehicle to rotate counter-clockwise).

3. Algorithmic Flow & Pseudocode:

function MODEL-BASED-REFLEX-AGENT(percept) returns an action
  persistent: state, the agent's current conception of the world state
              model, a description of how the next state depends on current state and action
              rules, a set of condition-action rules
              action, the most recent action, initially none

  state ← UPDATE-STATE(state, action, percept, model)
  rule ← RULE-MATCH(state, rules)
  action ← rule.ACTION
  return action

4. Operational Trace:

  1. Sensors receive current percept $p_t$.
  2. UPDATE-STATE combines previous state $s_{t-1}$, previous action $a_{t-1}$, current percept $p_t$, and world physics model to synthesize new belief state $s_t$.
  3. Condition-action rules evaluate against the comprehensive belief state $s_t$ rather than raw sensor inputs.
  4. Selected action $a_t$ is dispatched to actuators, and saved into memory for the next cycle.

"Formulate the 8-Puzzle Problem as a State Space Search Problem. Determine the total permutations, reachable states using the Inversion Parity Theorem, and average branching factor."

Show Complete Step-by-Step Model Answer

1. Formal 5-Component Problem Formulation:

  • States: A configuration of the $3 \times 3$ grid specifying the exact coordinates of the 8 numbered tiles $\{1, 2, \dots, 8\}$ and the blank space (denoted $0$).
  • Initial State: Any designated starting arrangement of tiles on the board.
  • Actions: Operations defined by moving the blank space: $\{\text{Left}, \text{Right}, \text{Up}, \text{Down}\}$. (Moving the blank space simplifies operator design over moving 8 distinct tiles!).
  • Transition Model $Result(s, a)$: Given board state $s$ and blank action $a$, returns the new board state resulting from swapping the blank with the tile in the target direction.
  • Goal Test: Checks if the current board matches the target configuration: $$\text{Goal} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & \text{blank} \end{bmatrix}$$
  • Path Cost: Each step costs $1$. Total path cost equals the total number of moves in the solution path.

2. State Space Size & Inversion Parity Theorem:

  • Total unconstrained permutations of 9 tiles: $$\text{Total Permutations} = 9! = 362,880$$
  • An inversion occurs when any tile with higher value precedes a tile with lower value in row-major order (ignoring the blank).
  • Theorem: In an odd-width grid ($3 \times 3$), horizontal slides do not alter tile ordering; vertical slides displace a tile by exactly 2 positions, changing the inversion count by $0$ or $\pm 2$. Thus, every legal move strictly preserves the even/odd parity of the number of inversions.
  • Consequently, the state space is permanently partitioned into two disconnected graph components: $$\text{Reachable States} = \frac{9!}{2} = \mathbf{181,440\text{ states}}$$

3. Average Branching Factor Calculation:

  • Blank in 4 corners: 2 legal moves each $\implies 4 \times 2 = 8$ moves.
  • Blank in 4 edges: 3 legal moves each $\implies 4 \times 3 = 12$ moves.
  • Blank in 1 center: 4 legal moves $\implies 1 \times 4 = 4$ moves.
  • Weighted average branching factor across all 9 squares: $$b = \frac{8 + 12 + 4}{9} = \frac{24}{9} \approx \mathbf{2.67}$$

PRACTICE 1 • 8-QUEENS STATE SPACE REDUCTION

Compare the state-space sizes of the naive formulation, column-constrained formulation, and complete-state permutation formulation of the 8-Queens problem.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

1. Naive Unconstrained Formulation: Place 8 queens on any of the 64 squares:

$$\binom{64}{8} = \frac{64!}{8! \times 56!} = \mathbf{4,426,165,368\text{ states}} \approx 4.43 \times 10^9$$

2. Column-Constrained Formulation: Place exactly one queen in each column ($k=1$ to $8$):

$$8^8 = \mathbf{16,777,216\text{ states}} \approx 1.68 \times 10^7$$

3. Complete-State Permutation Formulation: Exactly one queen per column AND per row:

$$8! = \mathbf{40,320\text{ states}}$$

Conclusion: By enforcing row and column constraints directly into the state representation, the search space collapses by a factor of over $\mathbf{100,000\times}$!

PRACTICE 2 • 8-PUZZLE REACHABILITY THEOREM

Can the start state $S = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 7, 0]$ reach the standard goal state $G = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 0]$? Justify mathematically.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Compute inversions of Goal state $G$:

In $G = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]$, all numbers are in increasing order. Number of inversions $= 0$ (Even parity).

Step 2: Compute inversions of Start state $S$:

Writing $S$ in row-major order (excluding blank $0$): $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 7$.

  • For tile 8: followed by tile 7 ($8 > 7$) → 1 inversion.
  • All other tile pairs are in natural order.
  • Total inversions in $S = \mathbf{1}$ (Odd parity).

Step 3: Parity Invariance:

Legal tile slides can only alter the inversion count by an even number ($0$ or $\pm 2$). Therefore, an odd-parity state can never reach an even-parity goal.

Final Answer: Mathematically unreachable (impossible!).

PRACTICE 3 • BRANCHING FACTOR DERIVATION

Derive the average branching factor of the 8-puzzle and explain why blank moves simplify problem formulation.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

A $3 \times 3$ board contains 9 possible positions for the blank space:

  • 4 Corners: Blank can move in 2 directions (along adjacent edges) → $4 \times 2 = 8$ legal moves.
  • 4 Edges: Blank can move in 3 directions → $4 \times 3 = 12$ legal moves.
  • 1 Center: Blank can move in 4 directions → $1 \times 4 = 4$ legal moves.

Assuming the blank is equally likely to occupy any square across a random search trajectory:

$$b_{\text{avg}} = \frac{4(2) + 4(3) + 1(4)}{9} = \frac{8 + 12 + 4}{9} = \frac{24}{9} = \mathbf{2.67}$$

Formulation Advantage: Formulating actions as "move blank" $\{\text{Left}, \text{Right}, \text{Up}, \text{Down}\}$ creates an action set of size 4, whereas formulating actions as "move tile $k$" requires tracking which of the 8 tiles is adjacent to the blank, creating up to 32 cumbersome rules!

PRACTICE 6 • RATIONALITY VS OMNISCIENCE SCENARIO

An autonomous vehicle travels safely at 40 km/h on an empty road. A boulder suddenly dislodges from an unmonitored cliff face and crushes the car. Was the vehicle agent irrational? Explain.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Answer: No, the agent was completely rational!

Engineering Justification:

  • An omniscient agent knows the actual future outcome of its actions with supernatural precision. An omniscient car would have stopped before the cliff because it "knew" the boulder would fall. However, omniscience is physically impossible in real-world stochastic environments.
  • A rational agent maximizes expected performance based on its built-in knowledge, sensory percepts, and past experience. Traveling at the legal speed limit of 40 km/h with no prior percept of overhead cliff instability is the action that maximizes expected utility.
  • Rationality is judged on the quality of decision-making given available information ex-ante, whereas perfection in hindsight is the criterion of omniscience. Therefore, an unexpected catastrophic event does not imply irrationality.
