§ 1

Professional Software Development & Essential Attributes

Software Engineering is an engineering discipline concerned with all aspects of software production from early system specification through to maintaining the system after it has gone into use.

SOFTWARE ENGINEERING VS COMPUTER SCIENCE
  • Computer Science: Concerned with theories, mathematical algorithms, and foundational computation principles.
  • Software Engineering: Concerned with the practical problems of producing software: economics, project management, customer collaboration, team development, toolchains, and quality assurance.

The Four Essential Attributes of Good Software

Attribute Engineering Definition Practical Significance
1. Maintainability Software must be written in such a way that it can evolve to meet the changing needs of customers. Over 70% of lifecycle software costs occur after initial delivery during maintenance and adaptation.
2. Dependability & Security Includes reliability, security, safety, and resilience. Software should not cause physical or economic damage in the event of system failure. Essential for safety-critical medical devices, banking transactions, and aerospace control systems.
3. Efficiency Software should not make wasteful use of system resources (memory, CPU processing cycles, network bandwidth). Critical for battery-powered mobile devices and cloud microservices where compute costs scale with usage.
4. Acceptability Software must be acceptable to the type of users for which it was designed: understandable, usable, and compatible with other systems. Software with great architecture will still fail in the marketplace if the user experience is hostile.
QUICK RECALL
  • Software engineering focuses on practical, cost-effective delivery of reliable software.
  • 4 Essential Attributes: Maintainability, Dependability/Security, Efficiency, Acceptability.
  • Maintenance constitutes the majority of the lifetime cost of software.

Software Engineering Ethics (ACM/IEEE Code)

Software engineers do not simply apply technical skills; they carry ethical and social responsibilities. Software can directly impact human safety, privacy, and economic well-being. The joint ACM/IEEE-CS Code of Ethics establishes eight ethical principles.

Principle # Principle Name Core Ethical Mandate
1 PUBLIC Software engineers shall act consistently with the public interest, prioritizing safety, health, and welfare.
2 CLIENT & EMPLOYER Act in the best interests of their client and employer, consistent with the public interest.
3 PRODUCT Ensure that products and related modifications meet the highest professional standards possible.
4 JUDGMENT Maintain integrity and independence in their professional judgment; reject bribes or compromised audits.
5 MANAGEMENT Managers and leaders shall subscribe to and promote an ethical approach to the management of software development.
6 PROFESSION Advance the integrity and reputation of the profession consistent with the public interest.
7 COLLEAGUES Be fair to and supportive of their colleagues; mentor junior engineers and credit intellectual contributions.
8 SELF Participate in lifelong learning regarding the practice of their profession and promote an ethical approach.
QUICK RECALL
  • ACM/IEEE Code of Ethics contains 8 principles.
  • Top priority: Public interest ALWAYS supersedes client or employer demands!
  • Confidentiality, Competence, and Intellectual Property must be honored.

Three Landmark Case Studies (Sommerville)

Sommerville grounds software engineering principles in three diverse real-world systems, each illustrating different operational challenges.

Case Study System System Domain Core Architectural & Engineering Challenges
1. Insulin Pump Control System Embedded safety-critical medical device Monitors blood glucose levels and injects calculated micro-doses of insulin. Zero margin for error: overdosing induces hypoglycemic coma/death; underdosing causes organ failure. Requires formal verification, fault-tolerant fail-safe hardware backups, and rigorous plan-driven documentation.
2. Mentcare Mental Health System Information system / Clinical record database Maintains patient history and prescribed medications across regional mental health clinics. Primary challenge: balancing medical staff access in psychiatric emergencies with strict patient data privacy regulations (HIPAA/GDPR).
3. Wilderness Weather Station Distributed sensor data collection Autonomous weather sensor stations deployed in harsh, remote wilderness with solar/battery power and satellite communications. Primary challenge: power conservation, self-healing reboot mechanisms, dynamic reconfiguration, and autonomous sensor calibration without human intervention.
QUICK RECALL
  • Insulin Pump: Safety-critical, formal verification, fail-safe architecture.
  • Mentcare: Information system, privacy regulations vs emergency usability.
  • Wilderness Weather Station: Distributed, low power, autonomous fault recovery.
§ 4

Software Process Models

A software process is a structured set of activities required to develop a software system. All processes involve four fundamental activities: Specification (defining requirements), Development (design and coding), Validation (testing), and Evolution (maintenance).

1. The Waterfall Model (Classic SDLC)

A strictly plan-driven, sequential process where each phase must be signed off before the next phase begins:

1
Requirements Analysis & Definition: System services, constraints, and goals established with users.
2
System & Software Design: Overall system architecture and data structures established.
3
Implementation & Unit Testing: Code written and verified as individual modules.
4
Integration & System Testing: Individual program units integrated and tested as a complete system.
5
Operation & Maintenance: System installed, put into practical use, and bugs fixed over time.

Strengths: Highly structured, predictable documentation, clear milestones for contract management.
Weaknesses: Inflexible partitioning; late integration; disaster strikes if requirements change halfway through.

2. Incremental Development

Specification, development, and validation are interleaved. The system is developed as a series of increments (versions), with each increment adding functional features to previous releases.

  • Advantages: Cost of accommodating changing customer requirements is greatly reduced; early feedback from working software; rapid delivery of useful functionality.
  • Disadvantages: Process is not visible (managers need documents to measure progress); system architecture tends to degrade as new increments are added unless refactored.

3. Reuse-Oriented Software Engineering (Integration & Configuration)

Relies on integrating existing Commercial Off-The-Shelf (COTS) software components, open-source libraries, or cloud web services rather than writing from scratch.

QUICK RECALL
  • 4 Activities: Specification → Development → Validation → Evolution.
  • Waterfall: sequential, rigid, document-driven, best for stable requirements (embedded/safety-critical).
  • Incremental: interleaved, early feedback, lower risk of customer rejection.
§ 5

Coping with Change & Boehm's Spiral Model

Change is an inevitable reality in all software development. Software engineering processes incorporate two primary strategies to reduce the cost of rework: System Prototyping and Boehm's Spiral Model.

SYSTEM PROTOTYPING: THROWAWAY VS EVOLUTIONARY
  • Throwaway Prototyping: A quick prototype developed solely to clarify poorly understood requirements. Once requirements are clear, the prototype is discarded and the production system is engineered properly.
  • Evolutionary Prototyping: Starts with an initial working prototype that is repeatedly refined through customer feedback, eventually evolving into the final delivered product.

Boehm's Spiral Model (Risk-Driven Process)

In Barry Boehm's Spiral Model, the software process is represented as a spiral rather than a sequence of activities with backtracking. Each loop represents a phase of the process, partitioned into four distinct sectors:

Sector # Spiral Sector Name Activities Conducted in Sector
Quadrant 1 Determine Objectives & Constraints Specific objectives for the phase identified; constraints on the process and product determined; alternative management plans drawn up.
Quadrant 2 Identify & Resolve Risks Detailed risk analysis conducted; prototyping and simulation used to resolve identified technical, management, and cost risks.
Quadrant 3 Development & Testing A development model (Waterfall, Incremental) chosen based on remaining risks; software is designed, coded, and verified.
Quadrant 4 Plan the Next Phase Project is reviewed; decision made whether to continue with a further loop; next phase plans developed.
QUICK RECALL
  • Spiral model is strictly risk-driven: each loop incorporates risk assessment.
  • 4 Quadrants: (1) Objectives → (2) Risk Analysis → (3) Development → (4) Planning next phase.
  • Radius of spiral represents cumulative cost; angular dimension represents progress.
§ 6

Process Improvement & The CMMI Framework

Process improvement aims to understand existing processes and change them to improve product quality, reduce costs, or accelerate delivery schedules. The Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI) provides a staged architecture to evaluate organizational maturity.

Level # CMMI Maturity Level Characterization of Organizational Process
Level 1 Initial (Ad-hoc) Processes are unpredictable, poorly controlled, and reactive. Success depends entirely on individual heroics.
Level 2 Managed (Repeatable) Basic project management practices established (tracking costs, schedule, functionality). Repeatable across similar projects.
Level 3 Defined Processes documented, standardized, and integrated across the entire organization. Standard software process defined.
Level 4 Quantitatively Managed Detailed quantitative metrics collected for both process and product quality. Statistical process control applied.
Level 5 Optimizing Continuous process improvement achieved through quantitative feedback and innovative pilot technology insertion.
QUICK RECALL
  • CMMI 5 Levels: Initial → Managed → Defined → Quantitatively Managed → Optimizing.
  • Level 2 establishes project tracking; Level 3 standardizes company-wide; Level 4 measures with stats; Level 5 optimizes continuously.
§ 7

Agile Software Development & Extreme Programming (XP)

Dissatisfaction with the overhead of heavy plan-driven models led in 2001 to the Agile Manifesto, prioritizing working software, collaboration, and rapid responsiveness over rigid process documentation.

THE 4 CORE VALUES OF THE AGILE MANIFESTO
  1. Individuals and interactions over processes and tools.
  2. Working software over comprehensive documentation.
  3. Customer collaboration over contract negotiation.
  4. Responding to change over following a plan.

Extreme Programming (XP) Key Practices

  • Pair Programming: Developers work in pairs at a single workstation (driver and navigator). Continuous peer code review improves quality and shares knowledge.
  • Test-First Development (TDD): Automated unit tests are written before writing the code itself. Code is only considered complete when it passes all tests.
  • Refactoring: Programmers continuously look for improvements to code structure ("cleaning code smells") even if no new functionality is added.
  • Continuous Integration: Code is integrated into the mainline repository multiple times a day; automated tests immediately verify build integrity.
  • Small Releases: Releases occur every two to three weeks, keeping user feedback immediate.
QUICK RECALL
  • Agile values working software and responding to change over rigid documentation.
  • XP practices: Pair Programming, Test-First Development (TDD), Refactoring, Continuous Integration.

The Scrum Framework, Sprint Velocity & Burndown

Scrum is an agile framework that focuses on managing iterative development rather than specific programming practices. It provides a lightweight structure of roles, events, and artifacts.

Scrum Roles, Events, and Artifacts

Scrum Dimension Element Role & Responsibility
Roles Product Owner Represents the business and customer. Owns, prioritizes, and maintains the Product Backlog.
Scrum Master Servant-leader. Ensures Scrum practices are followed, facilitates meetings, and removes external impediments for the team.
Development Team Self-organizing, cross-functional team (typically 5–9 people) that designs, develops, tests, and delivers the increment.
Events Sprint Fixed time-box of 1 to 4 weeks during which a "Done" usable increment is created.
Sprint Planning Team selects user stories from Product Backlog and defines how they will be built.
Daily Scrum 15-minute daily standup answering: What did I do yesterday? What will I do today? Any blockers?
Sprint Review Demo of completed increment to stakeholders for live feedback.
Sprint Retrospective Team reflects on its own process to identify continuous improvements for next sprint.
Artifacts Product Backlog Prioritized master list of all features, enhancements, and bug fixes for the product.
Sprint Backlog The subset of product backlog items chosen for the current sprint plus technical tasks.
Increment The sum of all Product Backlog items completed during a Sprint meeting the Definition of Done.

Scrum Velocity and Release Burndown Calculation

A software product backlog starts with a total estimated scope of $\mathbf{180\text{ Story Points (SP)}}$. A Scrum team completes three consecutive 2-week sprints with the following completed story points:

  • Sprint 1: Planned 45 SP → Completed 40 SP
  • Sprint 2: Planned 45 SP → Completed 45 SP
  • Sprint 3: Planned 50 SP → Completed 47 SP

Calculate: (a) The total completed points, (b) The team's average velocity, (c) The remaining backlog, and (d) The estimated number of additional sprints needed to finish the release.

Step 1: Total Completed Points:

$$\text{Total Completed} = 40 + 45 + 47 = \mathbf{132\text{ Story Points}}$$

Step 2: Team Average Velocity:

$$\text{Velocity} = \frac{\text{Total Completed}}{\text{Number of Sprints}} = \frac{132}{3} = \mathbf{44.00\text{ SP per sprint}}$$

Step 3: Remaining Backlog:

$$\text{Remaining Backlog} = 180 - 132 = \mathbf{48\text{ Story Points}}$$

Step 4: Estimated Additional Sprints:

$$\text{Sprints Remaining} = \frac{\text{Remaining Backlog}}{\text{Average Velocity}} = \frac{48}{44.00} \approx \mathbf{1.09\text{ sprints}}$$
The project requires $\lceil 1.09 \rceil = \mathbf{2\text{ additional sprints}}$ (4 weeks) to fully deliver the remaining backlog items.
QUICK RECALL
  • Scrum Roles: Product Owner (backlog priority), Scrum Master (coach/remover of blockers), Team (delivery).
  • Scrum Events: Sprint, Planning, Daily Standup (15 min), Review (demo), Retrospective (process improvement).
  • Velocity = Story points completed per sprint; Burndown tracks remaining work over time.

Important Examination Questions

2-Mark Questions (Short Answer)

2M List the four essential attributes of professional software.

Answer: (1) Maintainability, (2) Dependability and Security, (3) Efficiency, and (4) Acceptability.

2M What is the primary difference between plan-driven and agile development?

Answer: In plan-driven development, all process activities are planned in advance and progress is measured against this formal plan. In agile development, planning is incremental and continual, and it is easier to change the software to meet evolving customer needs.

2M What are the four sectors in Boehm's Spiral Model?

Answer: (1) Determine objectives and constraints, (2) Identify and resolve risks, (3) Development and validation, and (4) Plan the next phase.

5-Mark Questions (Medium Length)

5M Explain the roles and ceremonies in the Scrum framework.

Answer: Detail the three roles (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Development Team) and the five standard ceremonies (Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective) as presented in Section 8.

5M Describe the five maturity levels of the CMMI framework.

Answer: (1) Initial (ad-hoc), (2) Managed (project tracking), (3) Defined (organization-wide standards), (4) Quantitatively Managed (statistical process control), and (5) Optimizing (continuous technological innovation), as detailed in Section 6.

10-Mark Questions (Long Answer / Comprehensive)

10M Compare the Waterfall model with Incremental development. Under what project conditions should each be selected?

Answer Summary: Compare both models across phase overlap, accommodation of requirement changes, customer feedback speed, documentation overhead, and architectural integrity. Recommend Waterfall for critical systems with fixed specifications (e.g. avionics, medical pumps) and Incremental for e-commerce, cloud software, and business applications with volatile requirements.

Practice Problems with Hidden Answers

PRACTICE 1 • PROCESS SELECTION

Which process model would you recommend for developing flight control software for a commercial airliner? Justify.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Recommendation: The Waterfall Model (Plan-Driven).

Flight control software is safety-critical. Requirements are thoroughly analyzed and regulated by aviation authorities before development begins. Comprehensive formal documentation, traceability, and sign-offs are mandatory for regulatory certification, and unexpected architectural changes during late phases would be disastrous.

PRACTICE 2 • SPRINT VELOCITY

A team completes 30, 34, and 38 story points in sprints 1, 2, and 3. How many 2-week sprints will it take to finish a 170-point product backlog?

Show Step-by-Step Solution
  • Total completed = $30 + 34 + 38 = 102\text{ SP}$.
  • Average velocity = $102 / 3 = 34\text{ SP/sprint}$.
  • Remaining backlog = $170 - 102 = 68\text{ SP}$.
  • Sprints remaining = $68 / 34 = \mathbf{2\text{ sprints}}$ (4 weeks).
